Community guide

Vibe Coding Responsable 4.0: anything published openly must be reusable

Version 4.0 of Juan José de Haro’s guide explains that the educational exception in copyright law does not cover what is published on an open website.

Juan José de Haro has published version 4.0 of the guide Vibe Coding Responsable, a set of recommendations for teachers who create educational materials with the help of artificial intelligence. The guide, licensed under CC BY-SA 4.0, has already appeared in the paper, in the repository’s weekly round-up of 7 November, which listed it among the new releases.

The change its author highlights in the Vibe Coding Educativo group affects teachers who share materials. The copyright exception that the European Union provides for teaching, according to the chapter “Antes de publicar: diez recomendaciones” (“Before publishing: ten recommendations”), covers only use under the school’s responsibility, in the classroom or on closed platforms. Material published openly falls outside it, and so everything it contains must allow reuse: it cannot include protected images or texts.

This has a practical consequence for teachers who code with the help of AI: the AI assistant may link to images from other websites without their licence being stated. The guide recommends replacing each one with another image under a known licence and storing it inside the material itself. If an element does not allow reuse, or its licence is unknown, the prudent course is to change it.

According to the author, the change is based on the European Commission’s guidelines on digital educational content, “Making informed decisions about digital educational content”. That document is in the ChatGPT-IA-edu digital library. The guide’s page does not say what else has changed in version 4.0.

OECD and EU framework

OECD and EU set out four areas of AI literacy for primary and secondary schools

The non-binding AILit framework describes in 19 competences, with three levels of progression and classroom examples, what students should know, do and value when it comes to artificial intelligence.

The document

Title
Cómo preparar a los alumnos para la era de la IA: Marco de competencias sobre IA para la educación primaria y secundaria (original: Empowering learners for the age of AI: An AI literacy framework for primary and secondary education)
Authors
OECD / European Union (European Commission), with CodeAI
Year
2026
Language
Spanish (translation of the English original)
Licence
Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0)

Read the document: at its official source · in the ChatGPT-IA-edu Digital Library

“Empowering learners for the age of AI” is the title of the AILit Framework, a joint initiative of the European Commission and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). It was produced with the support of CodeAI, an AI education organisation, and a group of twelve experts from several countries. The European Commission presented it in Brussels on 18 June 2026; the document notes that it builds on a draft from May 2025, later revised after an international consultation.

The starting point is a distinction the text underlines: “AI literacy is not the same as using AI tools”. It takes an understanding of how these systems work, a critical evaluation of their outputs and a decision about when to use them, including the decision not to use them. Among its basic ideas are that an AI system is not human, that it generates results from statistical patterns without understanding or intent, and that students should describe it without attributing human traits to it.

The framework organises learning into four areas, presented in order: engaging with AI, creating with AI, managing AI and designing AI. The first (seven competences) is about recognising where AI is present, judging whether an output should be accepted, revised or rejected, and analysing bias and the use of energy and resources. In “creating” (four competences) AI is a collaborator that does not replace the student's own ideas, and authenticity and intellectual property are addressed. “Managing” (four) means deciding whether AI is the right tool for a task and dividing the work between people and machines. “Designing” (four) is the most technical: investigating who a system is designed for, evaluating it against criteria and proposing improvements. The text itself warns that this last area may need more time and support.

Each competence is broken down into knowledge, skills and attitudes, and into expectations at basic, intermediate and advanced levels which, according to the document, do not correspond to specific ages or school years. The classroom examples are concrete. In “AI or not AI”, students sort devices and apps according to whether or not they use AI. In another activity they move to corners of the classroom labelled “AI only”, “With AI help”, “Humans only” or “I don't know” and defend their choice.

The document does not hide its doubts. It cites data from TALIS 2024, the OECD's international survey of teachers: on average, only one in three teachers uses AI and three in four say they lack the knowledge to teach with it. It acknowledges that the evidence on the effectiveness of generative AI for learning “remains mixed” and that students who use it may produce better work without making lasting progress.

It has clear limits. It is not binding, its views do not necessarily reflect the official positions of the EU and it does not set out to guide how to apply the AI Act. More than 2,000 people from more than 100 countries took part in the consultation on the draft (from May to October 2025). The paper has already covered the conclusions of the Council of the EU on AI competences and the Canadian AI literacy initiative, which deal with the same subject. The library also includes DigComp 3.0, the European digital competence framework on which this one builds, and an earlier entry with the same title, from 22 June 2026.

For a teacher, the most useful parts are the sheets for each competence, with activities ready to adapt to any subject, and the sections devoted to schools and families. One way to start is to read the four areas and pick a single competence, such as evaluating AI outputs, to try out a classroom example. The licence is CC BY 4.0, with specific conditions for translations and adaptations.

EU guidelines

Eight EU criteria for choosing and evaluating digital educational content in the classroom

A non-binding European Commission guide supports teachers and school leadership teams from selecting a resource to evaluating its effect.

The document

Title
Toma de decisiones fundamentadas sobre contenidos educativos digitales: Directrices de la UE para docentes y educadores
Authors
European Commission, Directorate-General for Education, Youth, Sport and Culture (Publications Office of the European Union)
Year
2026
Language
Spanish
Licence
Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0), according to the document itself

Read the document: at its official source · in the ChatGPT-IA-edu Digital Library

“Making informed decisions on digital educational content” (title translated from the Spanish edition) is a guide from the European Commission's Directorate-General for Education, Youth, Sport and Culture, published by the EU Publications Office. It was produced with a group of experts from publishers, education technology companies, trade unions, universities and European bodies, with the manuscript completed in October 2025. It defines digital educational content as any educational resource in digital format. It is aimed mainly at teachers and school leadership teams, does not define curricula and warns that it is not binding.

The guide has three parts. The first explains what this content is for: reinforcing an explanation, working in small groups, practising, creating or assessing. The advantages it cites are accessibility, differentiating work according to level, immediate feedback in assessments, quick updating and analysis of usage data.

At the heart of the document are eight quality criteria, which according to the text synthesise 65 quality frameworks and academic documents: pedagogical suitability and alignment with the curriculum, engaging learning experiences, design, accessibility and inclusion, reliability, impact and evaluation, legal compliance, technical interoperability and financial viability. Each one includes what to look for in the classroom and in the school, a quick check and questions to ask. The guide suggests that teachers prioritise the criteria closest to the classroom, that the leadership team start with the financial, technical and legal ones and that everyone weigh all eight, for example with a radar chart.

Artificial intelligence appears throughout. Content generated with AI, the guide says, must be clearly labelled as such and used with care, and it is worth checking where its data comes from and whether it reflects the school's values. It mentions that providers must disclose when someone is interacting with an AI system, under Article 50 of the AI Act, and that works created entirely with AI, with little human input, are normally not protected by copyright. It recommends not feeding protected content into generative AI tools.

On the legal side, it asks schools to require a data processing agreement from the provider and to be wary of resources that ask for a camera, chat or links to unknown third parties. On licences, it warns that using a resource in an educational context does not mean it can be freely copied, adapted or redistributed. That the guide is not binding does not change what the General Data Protection Regulation or the AI Act require: these are rules the school must comply with, with or without it. In this same issue, Responsible Vibe Coding 4.0 applies the copyright exception for teaching to what is published openly; this article deals with the rest of the document.

The third part moves on to everyday decisions: selection, creation, access, use and adaptation, and evaluation, with steps, questions and fictional scenarios. On cost, it recommends calculating the total cost, including licences, training and support, and asking how a free resource is funded. The products it cites are illustrative and do not imply endorsement by the Commission.

For a teacher, it is useful as a checklist before adopting a tool, and for a leadership team, as a basis for a school policy. The questions under “Ask yourself these questions” can be taken almost as they are to a department meeting. The library holds related documents, such as the EU ethical guidelines on AI and data for educators (2022), which this guide recommends consulting, and the principles for procuring digital educational platforms, which deal with a neighbouring subject.

Transcribe audio and video and create subtitles without the file leaving your computer

Transcribe turns recordings into text and subtitles with the Whisper model, inside the browser and without sending anything to third parties. It was created by Juan José de Haro.

The resource

Name
Transcribe: Herramienta Local de Transcripción de Audio y Vídeo
Authors
Juan José de Haro
Licence
AGPL v3

Open the resource · Its entry in the repository

Transcribe is a web app that turns an audio or video file into text and lets you download the result as plain text or as subtitles. It was created by Juan José de Haro and opens in the browser, with nothing to install. The page says no data is sent to third parties: everything happens in the browser itself, which matters with recordings of lessons or students' voices.

Using it is straightforward. You press “Seleccionar audio o vídeo” (select audio or video), load a local file, choose the model (Whisper Tiny, Base or Small; Base is the default, “the best balance between speed and quality for most uses”, according to the page itself) and press “Iniciar transcripción” (start transcription). According to its author, the model is downloaded the first time, so the first transcription is the slowest. You can also import an SRT or VTT file to correct it.

The most useful part for a teacher is the editor. The text appears in segments with timestamps, which can be read and edited while the audio or video plays, and selecting a segment makes the player jump to that point. The page offers speed control, from 0.75x to 2x, and undo and redo buttons.

It can be used to subtitle your own videos and make them more accessible, to get a transcript of a recorded lesson or an interview, or to let students review a presentation in writing. Because artificial intelligence can get proper names, technical terms or unclear voices wrong, it is best to check the text before using it.

The interface is in Spanish, Catalan, Galician, Basque and English, with a selector in the top corner. According to its author, it keeps ten transcriptions by default, a number that can be changed. The code is licensed under AGPL v3 and, according to the repository entry, the app is aimed at secondary school, Bachillerato (the final two years of secondary school, ages 16 to 18), vocational training, university and adult and teacher education.

From the EduMedia archive

Ideogram lets you repeat the same character across several images for classroom materials

The image tool includes a free plan with weekly credits. On that plan, images are public, and students who are minors need their family’s permission.

Ideogram is an online platform for generating and editing images with artificial intelligence. In the EduMedia-IAG group, members shared its characters feature, which keeps the appearance of the same character across different images. According to the company’s own help pages, you add between one and five reference images and name the character in the description (prompt) using its tag; it can also be saved in the library for reuse.

In the classroom it can be used, for example, to create a mascot for a reading or a project and show it in several scenes: an illustrated story, a comic to revise vocabulary or a sequence explaining a scientific process with the same protagonist. Teachers can prepare the material and project it, or guide students as they create it.

To get started, you create an account, upload a clear image of the character or generate a first one, and then describe the new scenes. The help pages do not make clear which plans include this feature, and it may vary by workspace: it is worth trying it out before planning the activity.

Free accounts created with Google or Apple receive 30 credits each week, according to the help pages as of 24 November 2026; accounts created with an email address and password receive no credits. Images on the free plan are public, so they must not include students’ data or images. According to the terms of use, users must be at least 13, and those under the legal age of majority need a parent or guardian to accept the terms. It is preferable for the teacher to use the account.

A year ago Wednesday, 26 November 2025

Watermarks

Removing Gemini’s visible watermark was a paid perk; a year on, it takes one setting

In November 2025 it was surprising that Google let some subscribers remove the mark that flags their images as AI-generated. Since August 2026, almost any user can switch it off; the invisible mark stays.

On 26 November 2025, in ChatGPT-IA-edu, people were surprised that Google’s paid plans allowed the visible watermark to be removed from Gemini images, when the company itself championed SynthID, the invisible mark it uses to identify what its models generate. According to the Nano Banana Pro announcement, of 20 November, the visible star disappeared only for Google AI Ultra subscribers; the invisible one stayed on everything.

A year later, that privilege is just another setting. Since August 2026, according to Search Engine Journal and the Gemini Help page, users in most countries can switch the visible mark on or off for images, videos and music in the settings; in South Korea, India and Vietnam, only with AI Ultra. SynthID and content credentials (C2PA, metadata that indicates a file’s origin) always remain.

For the classroom, the consequence is clear: an image without the star no longer signals its origin at a glance. Gemini can check whether a file carries SynthID; in November 2025 it did so only for images, and it now covers video and audio, although it only recognises what was created with Google’s tools.
