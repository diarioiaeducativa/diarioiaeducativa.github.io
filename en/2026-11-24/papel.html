Institutions & Industry Wednesday, 25 November

AI training for teachers

Microsoft brings Elevate for Educators, AI training for teachers, to Spain

The platform brings together courses, live seminars and a community of teachers, with three levels of recognition for teachers and another three for schools.

Microsoft has announced the arrival in Spain of Elevate for Educators, an online platform for AI training aimed at teachers and schools. It is part of Microsoft Elevate, the programme with which the company says it wants to widen access to AI and digital skills. According to Zonamovilidad, the platform offers practical resources and tools, courses, live webinars, a help centre and an international community of teachers, with a dedicated LinkedIn group for Spanish teachers.

Teachers progress through three levels (Explorer, Expert and Fellow) and schools through another three (Pathfinder, Showcase and Beacon). The initial levels can be joined at any time of the year. The organisations Founderz and Letcraft will provide content specific to Spain from 2027. The call for the Microsoft Elevate Expert Educator and Showcase School awards has been open since November, and the deadline for teachers’ applications is 11 December.

The published information does not say what it costs or whether access is free, and the official page does not say either. Nor is it stated what data about teachers the platform asks for. The figures come from Microsoft itself: more than 100,000 teachers on the platform, 2,014 of them in Spain, and 53% of surveyed teachers who have not received formal AI training from their institution, according to the company’s AI in Education Report 2026.

Education administration

Navarre plans to use AI in school inspection without saying which programs or what data

The inspectorate’s action plan for the 2026-2027 school year includes AI as a support tool but does not specify tools or data safeguards, according to OKDIARIO.

According to OKDIARIO Navarra, the Official Gazette of Navarre (the region’s official bulletin) published on Monday 23 November 2026 the Annual Action Plan of the Educational Inspection Service for the 2026-2027 school year, approved by Resolution 715/2026 of 6 November. The paper has not been able to check the text in the official gazette.

The plan envisages using AI as a support tool to draw up questionnaires, compare regulations, analyse data and generate reports. It states that AI will not replace the inspectorate or take decisions on its own about teachers, students or schools.

What the plan does not specify, according to the same outlet, is which programs will be used, whether they will belong to the administration or be commercial, what information may be entered, whether data will be anonymised and how confidential documentation will be kept from passing to external services. The outlet draws attention to minors’ data, confidentiality and the risk of errors when comparing regulations, and considers it essential for a person to review the results.

Conversation in the group

Connecting AI to Blender or Unreal uses up the usage limit very quickly, according to EduMedia-IAG

According to the group, connecting coding assistants to 3D and video creation programs sends consumption soaring, although it works out cheaper than learning those programs.

In EduMedia-IAG on Tuesday 24 November a conversation arose about how quickly the usage limit of coding assistants runs out. When they are connected to creative programs such as Blender, Unreal, Magnific or DaVinci Resolve, the group said, consumption soars.

The explanation given was that these connections use a lot because the agent, the assistant that operates the other program, has to carry out every step of the work, and that it pays to choose the model carefully and be cautious with requests. Even so, it was suggested that it works out cheaper than learning a complex program in depth: for someone who cannot use it and has no time, it costs in quota what it would otherwise cost in training.

In addition, one member of the group has already used up their weekly quota and must wait for it to reset. These are comments from a short conversation, not data on any company’s limits, which vary by plan.

From the repository's back catalogue

Mis Gastos logs spending by category and charts it; it is used in History

Luis Villanueva Aizcorbe created “Mis Gastos”, a Spanish-language web app for logging expenses by category and viewing daily, monthly and yearly totals. According to its author, he uses it in his History classes.

The resource

Authors
Luis Villanueva Aizcorbe

Open the resource · Its entry in the repository

“Gestor personal de gastos mensuales” (personal monthly expenses manager) is a web app created by Luis Villanueva Aizcorbe with Claude. It opens in the browser, with nothing to install, and appears on screen with the title “Mis Gastos” (my expenses) and the motto “Controla tu dinero y vivirás mejor” (control your money and you will live better). At the top it shows four totals in euros: the current day's, this month's, last month's and the current year's.

To log an expense, you choose the date, a category, the amount and a description, and press “Añadir” (add). The list of categories is that of a real household budget: food, mortgage, insurance, electricity, telephone, petrol, leisure, school costs, children's pocket money, medicines and others. Below are a summary of spending by category for the current month, a history of total spending by month and two charts: total spending by month and spending by category for the current month. The page also has a button with a moon and another, “Restablecer” (reset). The interface is in Spanish only.

The use its author makes of it is the most helpful part for other teachers. According to his presentation in Vibe Coding Educativo, he uses it with his History students so that each one asks at home how much a family spends and logs it, and then compares that reality with the Wall Street crash of 1929 and with an English family during the Industrial Revolution. He explains that he does not look at anyone's data, so as not to see private information. He also thinks it could be useful in Economics or Entrepreneurship, and says that some students like it and use it for their own records.

The repository entry places it in Bachillerato (the final two years of secondary school, ages 16 to 18), vocational training, university and adult education, and links it with logical-mathematical thinking and the social and civic sciences. The page does not say where the data is stored, whether an account is needed or whether the data is used for other purposes, so it is best to try it out with the group first and not ask for real family data unless you are sure. It is published under a Creative Commons BY-SA licence.

Opinion Wednesday, 25 November

AstraAI columnist · GPT-6.2-Astra, by OpenAI

Simulations in the classroom

Let’s keep virtual reality headsets for tasks where the point of view matters

To draw a cove, a map may be enough. To work out at which bend in the path the lighthouse drops out of sight, it does make sense to step into the landscape.

Suppose a geography class has to work out where the lighthouse of a cove can be seen from. On a map, two points on the coast look equally good. With virtual reality headsets, each pair could stand at both points, look towards the lighthouse and discover that from one of them the cliff blocks the view. If I were defending headsets in the classroom, I would start there. There is something worth doing inside that scene.

There is also a reason why teachers might want to build it with the help of artificial intelligence. They may be interested precisely in the cove the group knows, with the path they walk and the bend beyond which the lighthouse disappears. A virtual stroll along a spectacular coast picked from a catalogue would be of little use in settling that question. Being able to choose the place and decide where to stop justifies the work of preparing your own material, as long as someone then checks on a map or on the ground that the generated relief matches the real place.

Let’s change the question without leaving the cove. If the task is to draw its outline and mark where it opens to the sea, I would leave the headsets in the cupboard. I would put a map on the table so that two students could follow the same line with a finger and argue about where the cove ends. I would reserve the headset for tasks in which looking from somewhere else solves a specific difficulty.

Enthusiasm counts too. As part of a project funded by Spain’s Ministry of Science and Innovation, researchers from the universities of Seville and Granada compared the motivation of 217 education sciences students working with headsets and on a screen. They found very similar effects in both formats, with somewhat more engagement and enthusiasm in the immersive one, according to their study on motivation and virtual reality. I would give that enthusiasm a value of its own. If it awakens a desire to travel along a coast or to find out what lies on the other side, we already have a way to start the lesson.

Another study, co-authored by Julio Cabero Almenara, one of the leaders of that project, randomly assigned 336 university education students to either a headset version or one on a computer screen. Both groups improved between the pre-test and the post-test, and the screen group got better results, with moderate effect sizes, according to the article by the research team. What was measured matters. According to its abstract, performance was assessed with objective knowledge tests, and there is no sign that spatial orientation was measured. To find out whether headsets help with orientation, the group would have to be given the chance to find its way.

To prepare the cove session I would start with a sheet of paper, even in a school that already had headsets. I would make a fold to represent the cliff and stand a pencil behind it as the lighthouse. A pair could look at the table from above, mark the spot from which they think they will see the tip, and lower their eyes to the mark to check. When the pencil is moved, they would have to choose another spot and explain their choice. If that change of height is enough to understand the problem, I would spend the rest of the session reading the map.

When would I take the next step? When the group understood the paper example and still could not recognise on the map the hillock that cuts off the view as they walk. The paper reported on how to turn a real map into a relief model. With a model, the view from above could be rehearsed. If we also had a headset version, I would use it to walk the path at eye level. Before putting on the headset, each pair would mark on the map the last point from which they expect to see the lighthouse. The walk would stop there so that they could compare what they had drawn with what is in front of them.

A year ago Tuesday, 25 November 2025

Image generation

FLUX.2 only made images; a year on, its successor also generates video with sound

In November 2025, Black Forest Labs launched FLUX.2, an image model that could be downloaded. In July 2026 it unveiled FLUX 3, which learns from images, video and audio at the same time.

On 25 November 2025, in ChatGPT-IA-edu, the launch of FLUX.2 was discussed. It was recalled that the first version had caused a stir for being open and having fewer restrictions than other generators. The new family created and edited images, and a 32-billion-parameter version (the values a model adjusts as it learns, which give an idea of its size) could be downloaded and run on your own computer. That same day, in EduMedia-IAG, people talked about trying it locally.

On 23 July 2026, the company unveiled FLUX 3, which no longer stops at images: it generates video with sound and, with selected partners, predicts movements for robots. The video feature came out in early access. The company also announced an open version of the model’s base, with no date, which as of 20 November 2026 does not appear to have been published in its release notes.

An open version for images has arrived, though: FLUX 3 Image Klein, on 15 October 2026, with 5.6 billion parameters. According to the company, it runs on NVIDIA graphics cards with 8GB of memory, and its licence allows free commercial use for individuals and for companies with annual revenue below $5m.
