Multimedia 3 November

Visual resources

A teacher recommends building infographics in HTML and SVG rather than as generated images

A member of Vibe Coding Educativo argues that, to illustrate concepts, AI should generate SVG shapes and icons in an HTML page, with generated images kept for when they are pedagogically necessary.

The proposal came up in a discussion of using vector illustrations instead of AI-generated images. Its author recommends asking AI to build the infographic as an HTML page with SVG shapes and a single family of icons, checking it thoroughly and, if needed, exporting it to PNG afterwards.

The advantages listed include sharpness; control for whoever creates it; consistency across resources, which makes it possible to set a shared “style book”; accessibility, because a screen reader can read the text of an HTML infographic but not that of an image without a description; and automatic adaptation to light and dark modes. HTML also supports collapsible components that reveal information in stages, which, in this view, helps avoid cognitive overload and adds a degree of interactivity.

Members also advised steering clear of infographics overloaded with decorative elements, generating images only when the teaching objective requires it, and always crediting the chosen icon family. Lucide, mentioned in the group, is an open-source set of SVG icons released under the ISC licence.

For teachers, the practical takeaway is knowing when an editable, accessible infographic is the better choice and when a generated image is.

Institutions & Industry 3 November

Higher education

MIT report calls for rethinking teaching and assessment in the age of AI

The committee set up by MIT to study the use of AI in teaching, learning and research training recommends AI-aware educational processes and a review of assessments and policies.

The report of MIT’s Ad Hoc Committee on the use of AI in teaching, learning and research training was shared in ChatGPT-IA-edu. According to MIT’s institutional page, the committee was commissioned in January 2026 and its final report was released on 25 August 2026.

The text proposes immediate and longer-term action along three lines: creating AI-aware educational processes, which means reviewing what students should learn and how they should achieve it, with changes to assessments and policies; keeping people, community and the on-campus experience at the centre; and putting in place the processes, teams and tools for continuous improvement, given the pace of change in AI. The institution also says that faculty will receive guidance for drafting their own AI policies.

For teachers at other levels, it is a reference for how a major university is tackling the issue: by reviewing assessment and policies rather than simply banning.

Debates 3 November

Teacher’s role

Teachers as designers of learning are at the heart of the debate on AI in the classroom

After a conference on AI and education, members ask whether teachers stop being a source of information and how to prevent students from delegating their reasoning.

One view holds that teaching and learning is undergoing a paradigm shift: teachers become designers of learning and guides, no longer the origin of information. Those who share it say they are concerned that students may delegate their reasoning to AI and that critical judgement, reflection and problem-solving need to be fostered.

Another contribution, in favour of that shift, frames it as a question for those who teach: do lessons spark enough curiosity for students not to turn to AI by default, and would it not be more fruitful to start from a research question than from an explanation followed by copying? It adds that AI is already a source of information for many teachers and companies, so what matters would be teaching students to discern critically.

A third contribution points out that, if students are going to live in a world with AI, they should already be preparing for it, and anticipates an interest in an “ethical agentic engineering” approach from an educational perspective. One of the contributions also raises a dilemma: using AI to create content while restricting it in students’ tasks.

Vibe coding 3 November

Security and agents

Giving a command-line agent full access means protecting students’ data

An experienced teacher explained what an AI agent with full control of a computer can do and what information should be kept out of its reach.

Asked what to consider before giving a command-line agent control of a computer, one member replied that with full access it can move and delete files, change settings, and install or uninstall programs. That capability is what makes it so versatile: in one case described, the agent built a complete project on a NAS from scratch and unaided, including obtaining a GitHub key to upload it.

The main caution concerns data. Students’ data is especially well protected and should not reach AI servers without knowing exactly what is being done and without the necessary permissions. Bank accounts and highly sensitive personal information are best kept separate. According to the person explaining, the agent itself can advise on how to protect that data from itself.

It was also noted that the agent can be restricted to a specific folder rather than the whole device, and an updated text was announced on the three ways of using AI: web, desktop app and command line.

Report published by UNESCO

A report for UNESCO proposes replacing “at what age?” with “for what purpose and on what evidence?” when using AI in the classroom

Sonia Livingstone and three co-authors argue that there is no single right age for using AI in education and call for decisions based on child development and clear educational aims.

The document

Title
Is there a right age for AI in education? Deciding whether AI is age-appropriate and why
Authors
Sonia Livingstone, Kim R. Sylwander, Sandra El Gemayel and Ayça Atabey
Year
2026
Language
English
Licence
not stated

Read the document at its official source

“Is there a right age for AI in education? Deciding whether AI is age-appropriate and why” is a research report by Sonia Livingstone, Kim R. Sylwander, Sandra El Gemayel and Ayça Atabey, published by UNESCO in 2026. Livingstone, professor of Social Psychology in the Department of Media and Communications at the London School of Economics, presented it at UNESCO’s Digital Learning Week and published a summary on the Media@LSE blog on 22 September 2026. According to that summary, the text was prepared to support policy briefs on the governance of AI in education. It should be noted that this article relies on the author’s summary and on the library entry, because the full text could not be opened in UNESCO’s repository.

The thesis is that there is no single right age, because child development varies and depends on context. Hence the proposal to change the question “at what age?” to “for what purpose, under what conditions and on what evidence?”, a formulation also found in the library entry. Livingstone calls for decisions to start from child development and clear educational aims; without them, she warns, education ministries risk making decisions without foundation. She also distinguishes between teaching about AI, that is, AI literacy, which she considers a priority from an early age, and using AI to teach, an area in which she argues there is little evidence. As a starting point in the face of that uncertainty, she writes that “the precautionary principle is a reasonable starting point”.

The summary offers a few data points. The UN’s Independent International Scientific Panel on AI looked for evidence on the effects of AI on society, including education, and found little on the benefits and more on the risks. In the Rights.AI research, carried out in Kenya, Brazil, India and Thailand, children used AI, often unsupervised, to find information, create, have fun and ask for advice. In the United States, 73% of teenagers said they had never talked to a teacher about what AI is and how it works. The author also mentions privacy risks and unannounced changes to the tools schools use, and notes that there are age restrictions in places such as Norway, New York and California.

In the library, the document sits alongside others that approach children and AI from different angles. The World Economic Forum report on child safety and well-being (our article) agrees that age alone is not enough as a criterion, although it is aimed mainly at those who design and deploy systems. According to the entry for the message itself, the text was presented at UNESCO’s Digital Learning Week, where other documents were published, among them “The Algorithm in the Room” (in the library).

For teachers, the value lies in the change of question rather than in a rule: before introducing a tool, it is worth knowing what educational purpose it serves, what evidence supports it and whether it is a matter of teaching about AI or teaching with it. The limit is that, by its own thesis, it offers no threshold that can simply be applied in a school’s rules. As a first read, the Media@LSE summary is short and links to the full report.

World Economic Forum report

The World Economic Forum sets out seven practices to make AI safe for children without shutting them out of learning

A September 2026 document from the Global Coalition for Digital Safety takes children’s well-being, and not merely the absence of incidents, as its goal, and proposes practices for those who design AI and incentives to ensure they are followed.

The document

Title
Children's Safety and Well-Being in the Age of AI: Best Practices and Incentives
Authors
World Economic Forum (Global Coalition for Digital Safety)
Year
2026
Language
English
Licence
© 2026 World Economic Forum. All rights reserved

Read the document at its official source

“Children’s Safety and Well-Being in the Age of AI: Best Practices and Incentives” is a World Economic Forum (WEF) white paper dated September 2026. It stems from the Global Coalition for Digital Safety, a Forum initiative, and the foreword is signed by Agustina Callegari, head of technology governance initiatives, and Cathy Li, head of the Centre for AI Excellence. The disclaimer explains that the conclusions are the product of a collaborative process and do not necessarily represent the views of the Forum or its members, and that generative AI tools were used to support drafting, with review by the authors.

It is not a classroom guide. It is aimed at those who shape children’s experience of AI: developers and model providers, app stores, regulators, families and also educators. It defines a “child” as anyone under 18, in line with the Convention on the Rights of the Child, and warns that this is a legal category and not a developmental profile. Hence its central thesis: there is no model that fits every case (“There is no one-size-fits-all model”), and the response cannot be reduced to blanket bans or age barriers.

The document argues that age alone does not make a service safe. Age verification is useful for applying differentiated safeguards, but it does not determine maturity, moderation or redress for harm. Nor does it accept that broad restrictions are free of cost: they can block benefits, reduce opportunities to acquire skills and push children towards less controlled services. It also points out that generative and agentic AI change the nature of the interaction, because they can sustain continuous exchanges, remember, personalise and act autonomously, and that humanlike language can lead minors to overestimate the system’s understanding or care.

The seven practices it proposes for organisations are: design for safety, age and development; encourage beneficial use while preserving one’s own judgement and human relationships; prevent harmful content, conduct and interactions, including child sexual exploitation and AI-mediated manipulation; protect privacy, dignity and informed understanding; strengthen learning, resilience and critical thinking; establish accountability, human oversight and continuous improvement; and promote inclusion, child participation and shared responsibility. Among the controls it mentions for minors are reducing, pausing or switching off an AI feature, declining memory or the reuse of conversations, and asking for a different explanation.

For education, its idea of learning is of particular interest: exposure does not create competence, and the use of AI “should not systematically displace the effort, feedback, discussion, play and social learning through which children build durable knowledge and skills”. It also argues that the test of a tool is relational: whether it helps the young person return to people, activities and decisions with more confidence, or whether its design prioritises itself as the preferred relationship.

The third part, on incentives, is what sets the document apart. It starts from the premise that protection will not be implemented consistently if it is costly or unprofitable, and proposes a portfolio of measures: comparable safety reports, procurement and distribution requirements, financial and insurance signals, shared testing infrastructure, stronger government capacity, safe channels for responsible disclosure and internal accountability tied to measurable outcomes for children. The authors acknowledge that this is a shared foundation and not a closed technical standard, and that the evidence on medium- and long-term effects is still limited.

In the library it is thematically related to the report for UNESCO on the right age for AI (our article) and to the narrative review “Inteligencia artificial y derechos del niño” (“Artificial intelligence and children’s rights”) (in the library). A teacher or school leadership team can use it as a checklist of criteria when evaluating or procuring tools: what controls students have, what data is kept, what happens when something goes wrong. It is best to start with the introduction and the first chapter (pages 5 to 10), which define the aim and the challenges, and continue with the section on learning and critical thinking in the second (page 16).

Official Catalan guide

Catalonia sets out 45 guidelines on which uses of AI to promote and which to limit in schools

The Department of Education and Vocational Training publishes a guide in September 2026 that advises against autonomous use of general-purpose AI by under-14s, grading with AI and AI-text detectors.

The document

Title
Orientacions per a l’ús de la intel·ligència artificial als centres educatius
Authors
Generalitat de Catalunya, Departament d’Educació i Formació Professional (Direcció General de Currículum i Desenvolupament Professional)
Year
2026
Language
Catalan
Licence
Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0)

The Department of Education and Vocational Training of the Generalitat de Catalunya published in September 2026, as a first edition, the Orientacions per a l’ús de la intel·ligència artificial als centres educatius (guidelines for the use of artificial intelligence in schools), drawn up by its Directorate-General for Curriculum and Vocational Development. It updates the 2024 document La intel·ligència artificial en l’educació. It was written by an internal working group between April and June 2026 and reviewed by a group of experts from the administration, universities, the inspectorate, data protection, school leadership and teaching. It runs to about seventy pages. The text itself states that AI models were used to review drafts and to write the examples in an annex.

The guide starts from the premise that students already use AI to study and do homework. In its words, the question is “not whether AI should enter classrooms or not, but how we want it to enter”. It justifies its caution with five reasons: AI solves a good part of the activities, AI-text detectors do not offer sufficient guarantees (especially outside English), general-purpose AI is not designed for learning, the available evidence is limited but points to risks if it is used without guidance, and there is no European public AI of reference, while US legislation (CLOUD Act, FISA) casts doubt on the real protection of data.

At its core are 45 numbered guidelines (OIA-01 to OIA-45), organised by area: school, teachers, educational services, support staff and families. Students under 14 should not use general-purpose AI autonomously; if it is used in class, the teacher operates it. Between 14 and 18, families’ consent is not needed, because the legal basis is the public-interest mission, but collecting their authorisation is recommended as a safeguard, compulsory when the tool’s terms require it, and direct interaction by students is placed from the second term of the third year of ESO (compulsory secondary education) onwards. It also asks for training and persistent memory to be switched off, agentic AI to be avoided, no personal data to be entered, and each school to approve a framework for use with a register of tools.

The pedagogical part is the most useful for the classroom. It asks teachers to keep the lead, verify what AI generates, declare their own use on a common scale and design activities that require interpretation and a personal position. In assessment, AI must not grade or determine the level of attainment, as this is considered a high-risk use under the European AI Act; it may help to create instruments, mark unambiguous answers or detect common errors using pseudonymised data. On detectors it concludes that they are inadvisable: “the best detectors are still people”.

Within the library, the text draws on frameworks already covered: DigComp 3.0, the UNESCO competency framework for teachers and the department’s own document on teachers’ digital competence in AI, whose basic stage it requires as a precondition. It cites the 2024 INTEF guide as state-level guidance; the library has its version 2. The age of use is also the subject of the document on whether there is a right age for using AI in education. The guide Tecnologies educatives al servei de l’aprenentatge: Educació bàsica, published the same month, cites it in its bibliography.

It should be read with its limits in mind: these are guidelines, not rules, and each school must adapt them; several sections refer to external pages that are not in the PDF; and the text admits that the evidence is limited and the technology changes quickly. What can be transferred outside Catalonia are the criteria, not the procedures. A good starting point is annexes 1 to 3, one-page infographics with general guidelines, for students and for families, and then guidelines OIA-28 and OIA-36 to OIA-38 on pedagogical use and assessment. Annex 4 offers four analogies (basketball, dance, rap, video games) to explain why learning is still worthwhile.

Official Catalan guide

Catalonia offers criteria for deciding when a technology adds value in primary and ESO, and places AI in the fourth year

The Department of Education’s guide puts technology at the service of learning, with decision tables, progression by cycle, digital well-being and criteria for selecting resources.

The document

Title
Tecnologies educatives al servei de l’aprenentatge: Educació bàsica
Authors
Generalitat de Catalunya, Departament d’Educació i Formació Professional (Direcció General de Currículum i Desenvolupament Professional)
Year
2026
Language
Catalan
Licence
Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0)

The Department of Education and Vocational Training of the Generalitat de Catalunya published in September 2026, as a first edition, Tecnologies educatives al servei de l’aprenentatge: Educació bàsica (educational technologies at the service of learning: basic education), prepared by its Directorate-General for Curriculum and Vocational Development. It runs to about thirty pages in Catalan under a CC BY 4.0 licence and is aimed at teaching teams in primary and ESO (compulsory secondary education). It is not an AI guide: it is a general framework for the pedagogical use of technology, set in the context of the Pla de digitalització responsable (responsible digitalisation plan) and the Catalan curriculum (Decree 175/2022). AI is dealt with mainly in one section, the one devoted to the fourth year of ESO.

The starting point is that digital competence is transversal: “Digital competence is a transversal competence that contributes to the attainment of all competences”. The guide breaks it down into five specific competences: information literacy, content creation, online participation and collaboration, safe, healthy and responsible use, and development of digital solutions. It insists that they are not independent blocks, and gives the example of an investigation that can activate critical searching, writing a report, collaborative work, citing sources and data protection all at once.

The most useful part is the table of pedagogical criteria, which turns nine criteria into decision questions: what learning is to be promoted, what the technology makes possible that would be difficult, less rich or less inclusive without it, whether it makes the process visible and not just the product, how it supports collaboration, what barriers to access it reduces, what risks and screen time need to be managed, and what evidence will show what has been learnt. A second table describes eight learning processes (researching, representing thinking, creating, collaborating, personalising, programming, documenting, taking part as a digital citizen) and the value technology adds to each. The conclusion sums it up: “The fundamental criterion is to use technology with educational quality.”

Progression by stage is gradual. In the first cycle of primary, use should be occasional, guided and brief, and throughout the stage it should not replace reading, handwriting, arithmetic, conversation or play; in the sixth year, specific attention is called for at the end of the stage. In ESO, real, interdisciplinary problems are set, and the guide states that the greatest degree of autonomy requires one device per student, in continuity with the model in the sixth year of primary. Each stage has a table with the degree of autonomy and sample digital practices.

In the fourth year of ESO the guide says that students can already begin to use AI tools “in accordance with the guidelines” of the department, with teacher mediation. It advises using it after students have thought through or produced a first draft, and not when it would replace the process, when evidence of independent work is needed or when students lack the prior knowledge to validate the answers. This is almost the same wording as the Orientacions per a l’ús de la intel·ligència artificial als centres educatius, which the bibliography cites and which also set the threshold at 14 and the second term of the third year of ESO. The text also draws on DigComp 3.0 for digital well-being.

The digital well-being section starts from the definition of the Council of the European Union and proposes seven dimensions (physical, cognitive, emotional, social, safety, inclusion and equity, and empowerment), each with actions for the school and the teacher. It closes with eight criteria for selecting resources, such as pedagogical relevance, accessibility, security and privacy, or quality of content. As for limits, it is general guidance: it provides no data or studies, does not recommend specific tools and leaves detailed agreements to each school. For a teacher, it is useful for using the criteria and selection tables as a checklist in a cycle meeting, and section 4.2.2 if they work with the fourth year of ESO.

AI video

A website gathers motion-graphics videos with their prompts and skills for agents

MotionVideo offers AI-made videos accompanied by their prompts, and skills that teach a coding agent how to do motion design.

motionvideo.xyz, a website of AI-made motion-graphics videos, was shared in EduMedia-IAG. Each video is published with its prompt, and the site also offers skills, reusable instructions that teach a coding agent motion design, with a link to GitHub. The videos are grouped by type, such as kinetic typography, charts, diagrams, particles and music.

A member of the group said they had adapted a skill designed for Claude to Gemini and, with a two-line prompt and a video from the site as a reference, produced the title sequence for a theatre teaser. This is a personal experience that could not be verified.

For teachers, the interest lies in having examples with their prompts for producing short introductory videos or videos that support an explanation.
