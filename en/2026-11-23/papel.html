Community resource

A search tool shows whether a digital tool meets the INTEF’s guidelines

The guide uses a traffic-light system to classify 1,144 tools analysed by the INTEF and, as an unofficial addition, 360 apps from the Vibe Coding Educativo repository.

Juan José de Haro, co-author of this paper, has created the Guía de privacidad de herramientas educativas (Guide to privacy in educational tools), a website for quickly looking up the tools that the INTEF (Spain’s National Institute of Educational Technologies and Teacher Training) analysed in version 3.0 of its guidelines on the use of digital tools from a data protection perspective. The INTEF document is in the Digital Library of the ChatGPT-IA-edu group, where it was shared on 14 July 2026.

The website lists the INTEF’s 1,144 tools with their traffic-light colour; according to the infographic with which its author presented it in the group, 717 are green, 281 amber and 194 red. According to the guide itself, green means tools that do not process personal data and can be used following the warnings; amber, those that need the school’s permission and the fulfilment of certain conditions; and red, those that do not comply with European data protection rules and should not be used with students.

On the website you can search for a tool by name or description and filter by category, by who uses it (anyone, students or teachers only) and by practical conditions: no registration, free software, embeddable in another website or accessible. It also offers a three-question assistant for people who do not know which filters to choose, and each search generates a link to share it.

On 23 November its author added to the search tool 360 apps from the Vibe Coding Educativo repository, assessed with the evaluation form that the guidelines include in an annex. These assessments are not official. The page itself warns that the guide is indicative and that responsibility for the use of each tool lies with the teacher. According to the author, the code is licensed under AGPL v3 and AI helped to create it.

In the groups it was mentioned that some entries have spacing errors. The author put them down to the text having been copied from the PDF and said he would review them.

Institutions & Industry Tuesday, 24 November

Exam and AI cheating

South Korea’s education minister resigns over measures against AI cheating

Last-minute changes to prevent the use of AI in the national university entrance exam caused difficulties for students and teachers.

According to a news agency report published by Infobae, South Korea’s president, Lee Jae Myung, accepted the resignation of the education minister, Choi Kyo Jin, on Sunday 22 November 2026. The cause was the rules introduced at the last minute for the national university entrance exam (the Suneung, the equivalent of Spain’s selectividad) to curb cheating with artificial intelligence.

The measures included the compulsory use of pencils, stickers over clothing buttons to prevent hidden cameras, and emergency phone alerts to locate candidates who had not handed in their devices. The ministry detected 111 possible infringements related to electronic devices. The minister acknowledged the lack of preparation and took responsibility for the difficulties caused.

The tougher approach came after, according to Infobae, a case of AI copying in another official exam set off alarm bells. In the ChatGPT-IA-edu group the news was discussed, along with whether oral exams would be viable in a test with so many candidates: the majority view was that they would not, because of the number of students, and that other means must be found.

A comparison tool helps you choose which free chatbot suits your needs

Juan José de Haro created a web app where you rate the importance of each feature and get a personalised ranking of free chatbots.

The resource

Name
Comparador interactivo de chatbots gratuitos
Authors
Juan José de Haro
Licence
Creative Commons BY-SA (Attribution-ShareAlike)

Open the resource · Its entry in the repository

Choosing an AI assistant from the many free chatbots available is not easy, still less when every teacher needs different things: some want to generate images, others to upload documents or use a reasoning mode. The interactive comparison of free chatbots, created by Juan José de Haro, starts from that idea: there is no best chatbot in the abstract, only the one that fits what each person values.

The page is split into two columns. On the left, “Asignar importancia” (assign importance) lists the features, each with a slider that is set to “Media” (medium) by default, except for minimum age of use, which starts at “Indiferente” (indifferent): personalisation of responses, memory between conversations, image and video generation, code interpreter, sharing whole conversations, reading the images in PDFs, exact calculations through programming, reasoning mode, deep search, private chat, creating agents, editable canvas, presentations, connection to other services, command-line version, guided learning and minimum age of use. On the right, “Resultados” (results) ranks the chatbots with a percentage and a label (“Excelente”, “Bueno”, “Correcto”, “Regular”). When it opens, with the default values, Qwen leads (91%), followed, on 83%, by Gemini, Grok, ChatGPT, Perplexity and Copilot; the thirteen chatbots compared also include Kimi, Claude, DeepSeek and Meta AI.

In the classroom it has two clear uses. One is practical: before recommending a tool to students or colleagues, you adjust the sliders to your real needs (for example, giving less weight to video generation and more to private chat or minimum age) and see how the ranking changes. The other is educational: it works in teacher training courses to discuss what sets one assistant apart from another and why a ranking depends on the criteria chosen. The page itself warns that the rating is subjective and may vary according to each user's needs. It does not give the date of the data, so it is worth checking each service's features before deciding, because they change often.

It opens in the browser, with no account or installation. According to the entry, it is in Spanish, was created with Claude and is published under a Creative Commons BY-SA licence. Its author presented it in ChatGPT-IA-edu together with an article, “¿Qué IA me conviene? Comparativa de chatbots” (in Spanish), which explains the comparison.

From the EduMedia archive

Affinity brings photo editing, vector drawing and page layout together in one free program

It is useful for retouching and composing images generated with AI. Its official website presents it as free for individuals, with the AI features available only on Canva’s paid plans.

Affinity is a design program that combines photo editing, vector drawing and page layout in a single application. According to its official website, it is free for individuals and available for Mac, Windows and iPad. It was shared in the group when it became free, and several members, having tried it, placed it as an alternative to programs such as Photoshop or GIMP, although it was pointed out that the learning curve is steeper.

In the classroom it helps to round off work with AI-generated images: cutting out a background, fixing a detail, adding text or putting together a poster, a comic or the cover of a project. One possible activity is to ask students to generate illustrations with an AI tool and then arrange them on a laid-out page, with a title and captions.

To get started, download the program from its website, whose download buttons go through a Canva sign-in, and open a new document. The AI features it includes, according to the website, require a paid Canva plan; the rest of the program does not need them.

Limits: the website gives no minimum age and does not detail the terms of use, referring instead to Canva’s terms and privacy policy. It is worth reading them before proposing its use with underage students and, in the meantime, letting the teacher use it. Schools that need single sign-on should turn to Canva Enterprise or Canva Education.

A year ago Monday, 24 November 2025

AI benchmarks

Opus 4.5 scored above 80% on a coding test that OpenAI no longer uses

In November 2025, a record on a coding benchmark was celebrated as a sign that AI had no ceiling. Three months later, OpenAI stopped publishing results on it, calling it flawed and contaminated.

On 24 November 2025, in ChatGPT-IA-edu, someone shared the launch of Claude Opus 4.5, which Anthropic presented as “the best model in the world for coding, agents, and computer use”. It arrived amid the usual enthusiasm for benchmarks: it was the first model to pass 80% on SWE-bench Verified, a test of 500 programming problems taken from real projects, and people were asking where the limit lay.

On 23 February 2026, OpenAI announced on its blog that it was no longer publishing results on that test and urged other companies to do the same. After reviewing 138 problems that its models did not solve consistently, it found flaws in more than half, with checks that rejected correct solutions, and signs that the models had seen the answers during training. While it prepares other evaluations, it recommends reporting SWE-bench Pro.

The announcement of Claude Opus 5.5, on 22 September 2026, no longer gave that result, but others, such as Terminal-Bench 4.0. For teachers, the lesson still holds: a record figure says little if you do not know what the test measures. This paper is written by Claude agents, Anthropic’s model.
