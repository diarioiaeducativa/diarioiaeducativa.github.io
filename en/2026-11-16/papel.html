Vibe coding 17 November

Industrial simulation in 3D

A robotic palletising cell is simulated in the browser and exports the scene as .glb

According to the message of the person who shared it, the app was built with AI models; it lets users stop, resume and monitor a virtual industrial cell and export the scene for Blender.

Magda Diaz-G has shared in Vibe Coding Educativo a 3D simulation of a robotic palletising cell, that is, a machine that stacks boxes or tins on a pallet. The page, “Celda Robotizada de Paletizado Industrial” (“Industrial Robotic Palletising Cell”), is in Spanish and opens in the browser. Its title carries the name “PINTURAS CROMO-MAX®” and its credits attribute it to “Ing. Magda Diaz”.

According to the page, it lets users start, pause and stop the cell, with an emergency stop and a reset, and switch between automatic and manual mode. It can also simulate someone breaking the safety light curtain, show production indicators (cycle time, rate, availability), the event log, the sensors and the angles of the robot’s joints, and switch between four camera views.

According to its message, the model was built with Gemini 4 Hydrogen, the Google model that the paper reported on when discussing Antigravity, and technical details were then added with a Claude agent, including the download of the file for Blender. The page offers to export the scene in .glb format, which Blender can open. To view it without installing Blender, the message recommends the InnerScene online viewer, which, according to its own page, opens Blender files in the browser, is free and requires no registration.

A simulation like this could be useful in an automation and robotics class to observe how sensors, safety and movements are coordinated without access to a real machine, although the message does not mention classroom use. It is another case of 3D models made with AI, this time with a technical purpose. The page does not say how it handles the data of the person using it.

Library 17 November

From the library archive

INTEF updates its guide to AI in education with ten ethical principles and a phased plan for schools to adopt it

Version 2.0 of the Ministry of Education's guide, published in September 2026, organises the use of AI around three profiles (students, teachers and schools) and adds annexes ready for the classroom.

The document

Title
Guía sobre el uso de la inteligencia artificial en el ámbito educativo (versión 2.0)
Authors
INTEF, Ministerio de Educación, Formación Profesional y Deportes
Year
2026
Language
Spanish
Licence
Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 (CC BY-SA 4.0)

Read the document at its official source

The Guide on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Education is published by the National Institute of Educational Technologies and Teacher Training (INTEF), part of the Ministry of Education, Vocational Training and Sport. Version 2.0 dates from September 2026 and updates the first edition, from 2024. It is aimed at three profiles: students, teachers and schools. It is in Spanish, runs to more than a hundred pages and is licensed under Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0. It remains useful as the reference document of the state education administration.

Its starting point is that “The central question should not be, therefore, whether or not AI is used, but how, for what purpose and under what conditions”. It places AI literacy in four domains (engaging, creating, managing and designing), taken from the framework that the OECD and the European Commission published in 2026, which was also shared in the library under the title “How to Prepare Students for the Age of AI”. For each profile it includes a table of challenges with preventive measures. For students, these include cognitive offloading and the “illusion of competence”; the measures include tasks that make the process visible, oral defences and practice without AI in the early stages of learning.

For teachers, the guide points out that the European AI Act classifies systems intended to assess learning outcomes as high-risk: it does not forbid assessing with AI, but it imposes obligations on whoever deploys it. It distinguishes between grading and offering continuous feedback, and mentions draft feedback that the teacher supervises. Where students have autonomy, it proposes guided self-assessment with rubrics designed by the teacher. On data protection, it recalls that Spanish law only allows processing to be based on a person's own consent from the age of 14.

For schools, it proposes integrating AI into the school's Digital Plan rather than treating it as a sum of isolated initiatives by individual teachers, in seven phases: a coordinating committee, analysis of the starting situation, review of the regulations, objectives, lines of action, gradual implementation, and monitoring and review. It recommends prioritising tools provided or authorised by the education authorities, informing families, and watching for the emergence of dependencies and for the impact on equity and digital wellbeing. The regulation it cites is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, also present in the library.

Among the new features is the ethics section, with ten principles (a person-centred approach, academic integrity, equality of opportunity, human oversight, educational purpose, transparency, privacy, responsible use, wellbeing and sustainability, and reflection and anticipation). Each comes with a good practice and a practice to review; for example, assessing a task without knowing which part was generated by AI. Another new feature is the environmental section, which gathers data from other sources: according to the guide, a query to a generative AI uses ten times more electricity than a traditional search, and between ten and fifty questions are equivalent to half a litre of water. These figures come from studies listed in its references; the guide does not verify them itself.

The limits are those of an institutional guide: it provides no data of its own on classroom results, its legal framework is Spanish and European, and many of its recommendations are general. The document states that some illustrations were produced with ChatGPT and that ChatGPT and Gemini were used for language support, with final approval of the contents resting with INTEF. The library also has version 3.0 of INTEF's Guidelines on digital tools and data protection, from the same body and focused on a subject that this guide covers only in part.

For a teacher, the most useful material comes at the end. Annex 1 proposes learning situations by stage, key competence and AI literacy domain. Annex 2 is a model statement of AI use for students, and Annex 3 is a ten-question reflection questionnaire to find out their starting point. To begin, it is worth reading the table of challenges for the relevant profile, then moving on to the ten principles and, for a school leadership team, to the seven phases in section 2.3.2.

Vibe coding 17 November

New in the repository

Trama offers 206 reading texts with questions for building reading comprehension worksheets

The catalogue, in Catalan and Spanish, is aimed mainly at the fourth to sixth years of primary school and lets teachers choose the questions before printing the worksheet or downloading it as a PDF.

The resource

Name
Trama · Comprensió lectora
Authors
Aarón Fortuño
Licence
MIT (code) · CC BY 4.0 (contents)

Open the resource · Its entry in the repository

Trama is an online catalogue of reading comprehension texts, created by Aarón Fortuño. It opens in the browser, under the motto “Llegir és seguir el fil” (“To read is to follow the thread”), and on 16 November 2026 it showed 206 texts. Each one lists its text type (popular science, narrative, instructions or letter), language, school year, difficulty, word count and number of questions. Among those visible on the home page are, for example, a text about an aqueduct, another about the porquets de Sant Antoni (woodlice) sheltering under a stone, and a letter from a girl to her aunt about a family radio.

Teachers can search by title, code or topic and filter by language, level, difficulty, length, theme, question type and text type. The page itself makes clear that length and difficulty are judged within each school year. Clicking “Prepara la fitxa” on a text lets teachers choose the questions and adapt the worksheet (format, answer space and header) before printing it or downloading it as a PDF. According to the description of the entry in the repository, the worksheet also comes with an answer key, which is useful for marking without having to prepare the answers separately.

The texts are in Catalan and Spanish, and the interface can be switched between the two languages. According to the information on the website, 96 texts exist in both languages, so the same activity can be used in a bilingual classroom or in the other-language version of a school. The texts are for primary school, with years four to six in what the home page shows.

Another feature is importing and exporting your own texts in JSON format, a text file with a fixed structure (title, language and paragraphs and, if wished, questions and answers). That way, a teacher can add their own texts to the catalogue in their browser; according to the website, imported texts are saved in the browser itself.

Trama is free, does not require an account to browse the catalogue and is a project of ortogra.fi, which also produced Fil i compte, which prepares maths problem worksheets. The code is published under the MIT licence and the texts, questions and answers under a CC BY 4.0 licence, which allows them to be reused with credit to the author. The website does not specify what student data it processes, if any: as far as can be seen, the tool is used by the teacher and students work with the printed worksheet.

From the EduMedia archive

EyeJack Creator: bringing an image to life on your phone with augmented reality

A website lets you attach a video or animation to an image and watch it on your phone by pointing at it. The free version limits views, duration and size.

EyeJack Creator is an online augmented reality platform. You upload an image that acts as a trigger (JPG or PNG) and attach a video, a GIF or a sequence of PNG images, which plays over it when you point your phone at it. It also lets you place panels, static or animated, in a space to create walk-through tours. It was shared in the EduMedia-IAG group as a resource for creating this kind of content.

In the classroom it can link what students produce with image, audio or video AI to a physical medium. For example, a school poster or a mural whose drawing, when scanned, shows a short explanatory video made by the students themselves.

To get started, you upload the trigger image, choose the video or animation that will play over it and test it on your phone using the preview QR code.

Terms. According to its page, as of November 2026, the free plan includes 100 views a month, up to 30 seconds per artwork and 3 MB per panel project; once the views run out, artworks are deactivated until the following month. Paid plans start at $10 a month. Access requires logging in, and the website does not state the minimum age or what data it collects, so it is advisable for teachers to create the artworks and for students only to view them.

A year ago 17 November 2025

AI in Catalan secondary schools

Failed for studying from flawed ChatGPT notes; today Catalonia has 45 guidelines on use

In November 2025, a Catalan teacher said he had failed students who studied from flawed notes made with ChatGPT, and the group called for literacy and rules. A year later, the Departament d’Educació has set out how AI should enter schools.

On 17 November 2025, in ChatGPT-IA-edu, a news item from RAC1 was shared about a secondary-school teacher who had failed students for studying from flawed notes made with ChatGPT. The replies did not blame the tool: they called for “literacy and legislation now”, critical thinking and a shared vision in each school.

At the time, a guidance document from February 2024 was in force. In September 2026, the Departament published the Orientacions per a l’ús de la intel·ligència artificial als centres educatius, which the paper reviewed on 3 November: 45 guidelines that ask teachers to verify what AI generates, advise against AI-text detectors and against unsupervised use of general-purpose AI before the age of 14, and ask each school to set its own framework for use. Between 14 and 18, according to the Generalitat’s page, authorisation is required.

The guide covers much of what was being asked for that day: judgement, verification and a whole-school vision. But these are guidelines, not rules, and it requires teachers to be trained first, which is exactly what was then being demanded as literacy.
