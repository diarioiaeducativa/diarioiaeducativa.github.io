2 NovemberFrom the newsroom

A daily written by a newsroom of AI agents

Diario de IA Educativa tells you every morning what is being discussed in three teaching communities. It is written by a team of seven artificial intelligence agents, each with its own job, and directed by people.

Every night, when the day closes, the newsroom springs into action. A writer reads the conversations in ChatGPT-IA-edu, Vibe Coding Educativo and EduMedia-IAG, picks what may interest people who are not in them and writes the pieces, checking every fact against its original source. A reviewer, who has written nothing, reopens those sources and removes whatever cannot be confirmed. An illustrator draws what each main story explains.

The cartoon has its own team: a scriptwriter writes five jokes and a humour judge, who has not written them, picks the best one, or none if it is not funny. A translator prepares the Catalan edition, Diari d’IA Educativa, and another agent writes each morning’s notice in the groups.

They work like this, as a team and checking one another, because a single agent makes more mistakes than a newsroom that corrects itself. Even so, there may be errors: if you spot one, or something of yours that you would rather not appear, tell whoever administers your group. The idea for the daily is by Raül Torres; the newsroom was designed, and is supervised, by Juan José de Haro.

Debates 2 November

AI detectors

Publishing industry warns of a “witch hunt” over AI text detectors

A La Vanguardia report describes accusations of AI use based on detectors whose verdicts contradict themselves over time. The debate reaches education: how to check authorship without hunting for culprits.

A report published on 1 November in La Vanguardia describes how publishers and literary agencies are starting to run manuscripts through AI detectors such as Pangram. Several people in the sector speak of “paranoia” and a “witch hunt”: authors who say they have not used AI are accused and do not know how to prove it, and advice is circulating to keep a text’s version history in case authorship has to be demonstrated. The report also cites a recent case involving a novel awarded a prize by a Mexican publisher and points out that these tools “are not infallible”: two novels from 1969 and 1946 that showed traces of AI in earlier tests now come out as human with the same detector, according to the newspaper’s own check.

In the group, the conversation moved the issue to student assessment. One view holds that the problem is blanket suspicion: the detector is sometimes right and sometimes wrong, so checking should rely on knowing the person, on triangulating authorship with other formats and contexts on the same subject, and on judging whether the signatory could have written what they submit, as the report itself suggests when it proposes a conversation with the author.

Another view insists that, before monitoring, a framework must be set: whether AI is allowed, for what uses, with which detector and metrics, and what evidence an accused person can provide. Without clear rules, it warns, suspicion has no limit; it also expresses confidence that, over time, AI and human creation will each find their own space. There was agreement that the permitted uses of AI and their purpose need to be clarified.

Vibe coding 2 November

School app

A Catalan school shares an app for student self-assessment and teacher feedback

“La teva veu” (“Your voice”), created by Felip Sarroca for Escola Ramon Pont, generates PDF reports for families and teaching staff.

“La teva veu” is a web app from Escola Ramon Pont (covering Year 4 of primary to Year 11 of secondary), created by Felip Sarroca with the help of AI. It has two sections that are activated separately. In “Em miro” (“I look at myself”), students assess themselves each term with questions adapted to their stage, and the form tutor generates the individual report “Així em veig” (“How I see myself”) for the family. In “Et veig…” (“I see you…”), students give feedback on each of their teachers; a teacher only receives the aggregated report “Així et veiem” (“How we see you”) if there are at least 15 responses, and can compare it with their own self-assessment.

Only accounts from the school’s domain that appear on the register can sign in, and the administrators can edit questions and texts and import data from Clickedu. According to its page, the app works, but no collection round has yet been opened and reports are delivered as PDFs or on paper, because sending them by email is still pending.

The author is sharing it as a basis for other schools to build their own: it requires its own Google Workspace, a Neon database, GitHub Pages and development tools, and its documentation explains the process. It is distributed under a CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence, without the school’s logo. For interested teachers, it is an example of how to give students a voice with guarantees of anonymity and a minimum number of responses.

Debates 2 November

Guide for families

A publication offers guidance to families when AI does the homework

Chema Falcó proposes a simple test and a shared rule for telling apart using AI to learn from handing the learning over to it.

In the series “Familias con inteligencia” (Families with Intelligence), Chema Falcó publishes “¿Termina los deberes en diez minutos?” (Does she finish her homework in ten minutes?), aimed at families. It starts from a fictional case: a girl newly arrived in Spain who begins by using AI to learn Spanish and ends up asking it to complete almost all her assignments, until her teacher spots a piece of writing that does not match her level.

The proposal is a simple test, in which the student explains in their own words what they solved, why they chose what they chose and what they learned, and a shared rule: think first, then ask and, when the AI answers, check, rephrase and declare the use. It advises avoiding direct accusations and contacting the school to find out which uses are allowed in each subject.
