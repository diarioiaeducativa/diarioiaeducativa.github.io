Cinema with AI

A tool turns a mobile phone and VR headset into a virtual camera for Blender

Raül Torres describes how, with help from Claude, he built a system that records real camera movements in Blender to then guide an AI video generator.

Raül Torres says he has used Claude to create a tool for controlling the camera in 3D scenes without relying only on the text of a prompt. The phone version consists of a web page using WebXR that reads the phone’s position and rotation, and a Python add-on for Blender that moves the scene’s camera in real time. When he presses REC and walks around the room, Blender records the keyframes; the phone screen shows what the camera sees.

Afterwards, according to his account, the recording is stopped, an MP4 is exported and that video serves as a camera reference for the Seedance 2.5 video generator. The official Seedance 2.5 page confirms that the model can take a reference video as a guide for camera movement.

A second version brings the same method to a Meta Quest 3 headset: using Quest Link and the VR Scene Inspection (OpenXR) add-on that comes with Blender, the person recording holds a virtual camera in the controller, the trigger starts and stops recording, and the joysticks allow tracking shots and pans. The author notes that he still has to fine-tune details, such as the speed of the left controller.

For the audiovisual or art classroom, the idea matters because it hands control of framing and movement back to students: they build the set, decide where the camera goes and AI is used afterwards to transform that material. The author gives his own series, “Never Sleep Again”, as an example of his way of working, which the paper reported on 1 November. The tools described are a personal development and, for now, the group has only his screenshots, test videos and explanation to go on.

Science in 3D

An open project offers 54 interactive 3D science lessons for upper secondary school, in Chinese

Tongjiang Lab brings together physics, chemistry, biology and geography lessons in HTML files that work offline; in the group it was pointed to as an example of what will be possible with AI.

Tongjiang Lab (铜匠实验室), an open repository of 3D science lessons for upper secondary school, was shared in Vibe Coding Educativo. According to its documentation, it contains 54 lessons: 10 in physics, 25 in chemistry, 5 in biology and 14 in geography. Each is a single HTML file of roughly 1 MB that works offline, so it can be used on a projector or an interactive whiteboard.

Each lesson presents a 3D scene with a live control panel, a guiding question and a step-by-step route for teachers. They can be opened online, downloaded one by one or as a zip, and embedded in another website via an iframe. The lessons follow the Chinese curriculum and the textbooks of the publisher People’s Education Press, although the project makes clear it has no connection with it.

The interface is in Chinese; the project says other languages are planned. The licence is CC BY-NC 4.0, which allows sharing and adapting with credit and without commercial use. The author is listed as Tongjiang.

The person who shared it in the group presents it as a sample of what will be possible in the coming months with AI, and has translated one of the simulations into English on their own website. The interest for Spanish-speaking teachers is twofold: a model of a self-contained 3D lesson and a reusable base, as long as the licence is respected.

New in the repository

A tool randomly forms the home and expert groups for the Jigsaw technique

“Rompecabezas – Técnica Jigsaw” takes a list of students and subtopics and splits them into balanced groups. It opens in the browser and is available in five languages.

The resource

Name
Rompecabezas – Técnica Jigsaw
Authors
Juan José de Haro
Licence
AGPL-3.0

Open the resource · Its entry in the repository

“Rompecabezas – Técnica Jigsaw” is a tool for organising jigsaw cooperative learning in the classroom. In this technique, each student becomes an expert in one part of the topic, then shares it with their group, and everyone needs the others to complete the whole. The app takes care of the heaviest part for teachers: forming the groups.

The start screen has two panels. On the left, you type the students’ names, one per line, or click “Cargar ejemplo” to try the tool with sample data. On the right, you choose the number of subtopics, which matches the number of members in each home group, give each one a name (the screenshot shows four, each in a different colour) and, if you wish, enter the general topic, for example “El aparato digestivo”. When you click “Formar grupos”, the tool assigns students at random, with the subtopics spread evenly.

According to the listing, when the number of students does not divide exactly, the extras are handled as co-experts. The listing adds that the app guides the three phases of the technique (home groups, expert groups and return to the home group) with prompts, a timer and a printable view. Once the groups are formed, the app also shows steps numbered 1 to 3 and a ten-minute timer with start and stop buttons.

The listing classifies it as a creation tool for Primary, Secondary, Upper Secondary, Vocational Training, University and Teacher Training, and for any subject, since it helps organise the class and does not depend on any particular content. The interface can be switched between Spanish, Catalan, Galician, Basque and English. It is used directly in the browser, and the start screen does not ask for an account.

It was created by Juan José de Haro. The code is distributed under the AGPL-3.0 licence, and the page links to a channel for reporting errors and making suggestions.

New in the repository

A block-based programming editor, built on TurboWarp and compatible with Scratch, for learning to code

EduTicTac Blocs is a visual programming environment compatible with Scratch projects and with classroom templates; according to its website, it has no advertising or trackers.

The resource

Name
EduTicTac Blocs
Authors
Samuel Soriano
Licence
GPL v3

Open the resource · Its entry in the repository

EduTicTac Blocs is a free, open block-based visual programming environment designed for education. According to its own page, it is compatible with Scratch .sb3 projects, is based on TurboWarp and carries no advertising or trackers. It also makes clear that it is not affiliated with Scratch, its team, the Scratch Foundation or TurboWarp. The listing places it in Primary, Secondary and Teacher Training, in the areas of Science and Technology and Logical-Mathematical Thinking, with an interface in Catalan and English.

When you open the website, an example project ready to run appears without asking for an account, with a “Ver dentro” button to switch to the block editor: at the top, the Ajustes, Archivo, Editar, Complementos and Avanzado menus, and the “Inicio” and “Entrar” buttons; in the centre, the stage with green flag, pause and stop controls. A notice invites users to click the green flag, type their name when the character Bloc asks for it and then click on it. Below, the “Empieza con una plantilla” button announces twenty ready-made projects, each with its own teaching sheet, and “Explora la galería” gives access to more examples.

For teachers, the appeal is that it allows them to introduce computational thinking with Scratch’s block logic and bring ready-made projects into class as a starting point. According to the listing, students can create projects with blocks, costumes, drawings and extensions, and there is an artificial intelligence assistant to generate them, though what its use requires is not detailed.

The listing also notes that it can be self-hosted using Docker, so a school or its IT coordinator can deploy it on their own server. The resource is listed as created by Samuel Soriano and its source code is published under the GPL v3 licence, with links in the footer to the privacy information and to the TurboWarp credits.
