Debates 4 November

Google and the classroom

Teachers debate Google’s announcement that switches off AI and search by default in school accounts

Google has announced that Gemini and Google Search will be switched off in primary and secondary school student accounts until administrators turn them on. In the groups, the news sparked a discussion about who decides how AI is used in a school.

According to Engadget, which cites Chris Phillips, Google’s vice-president and general manager for education, the company announced on 3 November that access to Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search will be switched off in primary and secondary (K-12) school accounts until administrators explicitly turn it on. Google clarified to the outlet that each product can be enabled separately and that it is not an all-or-nothing option. The new controls will roll out over the coming months.

The news reached the group first as an alarmist social-media version (“schools that ban AI will lose Google Search”). Members asked for it to be checked, and Engadget’s clarification was then shared. According to the same outlet, students at a school that blocks AI will not have access to Search and will see a message from their school saying so.

Several positions were put forward in the discussion. One welcomes AI being a decision for the school, and switching it off entirely if the school so chooses, for consistency with not allowing other AI chatbots. Another fears that access to information will be made conditional on accepting AI, and recalls that such access is a right. A third places digital coordinators between Google’s offer and caution about younger students, and awaits new guidance from the Catalan education department. Members also asked that teachers be able to monitor what students type, and argued for training teachers and families first, without rushing.

The Departament d’Educació’s guidance on the use of AI in schools was published in September 2026, and the newspaper reviewed it in its Library section, although the project page on XTEC still describes it as “under development”.

Community resource

A gamified activity takes the gravitational field in Year 13 Physics to nine orbital missions

“Misiones orbitales”, by El profe de la bata, asks students to create an astronaut and complete nine missions using interactive simulators. The practice version runs in the browser, with no accounts.

“Misiones orbitales” is a Physics activity for the second year of Spanish Bachillerato (Year 13) on the gravitational field, created by El profe de la bata. Students create their astronaut and complete nine missions; for each one they consult interactive simulators, from which they obtain the data needed to calculate orbital and escape velocities, apply Kepler’s laws, find orbital energy or deduce the mass of a star.

There are two versions. The practice version runs directly in the browser, with no accounts and unlimited attempts, although it is not intended for grading. The classroom version is installed with Google Apps Script, uses Google Workspace accounts, allows one attempt per mission, saves progress in a teacher’s spreadsheet and includes a dashboard for projecting. Scores are expressed in stars according to the error made: below 5% earns all of them.

The project is distributed under a CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence.

MIT report

An MIT committee calls for rethinking aims, assessment and the in-person community in the face of an AI that can already do almost any written work

The ad hoc committee of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology proposes eight principles and a set of recommendations for adapting university teaching to generative AI.

The document

Title
Report of MIT's Ad Hoc Committee on AI Use in Teaching, Learning, and Research Training
Authors
Ad Hoc Committee on AI Use in Teaching, Learning, and Research Training, Massachusetts Institute of Technology (co-chairs: Eric Klopfer and Sam Madden)
Year
2026
Language
English
Licence
not stated

Read the document at its official source

The document is the final report of the Ad Hoc Committee on AI Use in Teaching, Learning, and Research Training at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). The institution’s leadership commissioned it in January 2026 to assess current use of generative AI, identify innovations in teaching and assessment, and propose a policy on its use. It is dated 13 August 2026 and is co-chaired by Eric Klopfer and Sam Madden; the committee brings together undergraduate and graduate students, faculty from all of the schools and staff from units such as the libraries and the Teaching and Learning Lab. It is an institutional document, addressed to the MIT community.

The committee concludes that the issue goes deeper than drafting a rule: it affects the structure, purpose and value of a university education. According to the report, AI can give reasonable answers to almost any written task in the undergraduate curriculum, and it warns: “This is not a moment for patches and duct tape.” Among the data it provides, in the autumn 2025 technology survey more than two thirds of the students who responded believed that AI would matter in their careers, but only 25% felt that MIT was adequately preparing them to use it.

The eight principles are: be humble, be bold, put people at the centre, bet on learning, teach with intention, recognise that there is no single solution, augment rather than automate, and think beyond the classroom and the campus. The fourth speaks of productive effort and a new social contract between teachers and students; the seventh asks for AI to be used, in the report’s words, “to augment and enhance curiosity, creativity, and learning, not automate them”. The fifth proposes defining the purpose of learning first, rather than asking whether AI should be allowed or banned.

The recommendations are grouped into three blocks. The first, on educational processes, calls for a review of the aims of each subject, the use of oral exams, semester portfolios or out-of-class work accompanied by discussion in class, and stronger project-based learning and in-person social learning. It also calls for a justified AI policy in every course, in a standard format with clear and consistent guidelines. It advises against relying on AI detectors, which it considers unreliable, and describes current lockdown browsers as flawed and close to surveillance, although it recommends studying them; it prefers version history and staged feedback. The second block, on community and the residential experience, recommends that faculty disclose their own use of AI, that effective, responsible and ethical use be taught from students’ induction sessions onwards, and that every thesis include a statement on how AI was used, without it ever being listed as a co-author.

The third block proposes support structures: a standing committee on AI and education, AI leads in each school or department, fellows and an implementation team, a fund for pilots, ongoing training and support for faculty, and metrics on use, participation and outcomes. It also includes equitable access to technology, a platform that does not tie the community to a single model provider, and monitoring of costs and environmental impact. These measures depend on the resources of a particular university.

This report is thematically related to other documents in the library: assessment in the age of AI, such as Responsible Assessment in the AI Era, or academic integrity, such as A Comprehensive Academic Integrity (CAI) Framework: An Overview. The Diario has not compared their content with that of this report. For a teacher, the most useful parts are the transferable ideas: start from the learning objectives before deciding what is permitted, state in each course which uses of AI are acceptable and why, and diversify assessment with oral or process-based components. It is best to begin with the block on educational processes, the one closest to the classroom. As a limit, the context is that of a particular research university, so applying it to other levels or schools requires adaptation.

New in the repository

A Catalan-language lab lets students practise inorganic formulation with questions, hints and challenges

“Laboratori de formulació”, created by Felip Sarroca i Gil, offers inorganic nomenclature exercises for Secondary and Upper Secondary students, with three working modes and a progress counter.

The resource

Name
Laboratori de formulació
Authors
Felip Sarroca i Gil
Licence
CC BY-NC-SA 4.0

Open the resource · Its entry in the repository

“Laboratori de formulació” is a web app for learning and practising the formulation and nomenclature of inorganic compounds. According to the repository listing, it follows the 2005 IUPAC recommendations and is aimed at students in Compulsory Secondary Education and Upper Secondary, in the area of Science and Technology. It is entirely in Catalan, and the footer states that Felip Sarroca created it with the assistance of artificial intelligence.

On opening, the header shows three modes: “Aprèn”, “Practica” and “Repte”. In practice mode, which is the one shown in the screenshot, a multiple-choice question appears with four answers, for example the formula for barium sulphide, with the compound family label (“sal binària”) and the level (“fàcil”). Below are buttons to ask for a hint or show the solution. Users can filter by level (easy, medium and Upper Secondary) and by compound family, and the side panel lets them choose the type of activity: a mix of all, building compounds by joining ions until the total charge is zero, going from name to formula, from formula to name, choosing among four options or classifying a compound into its family.

A counter of attempts, correct answers and streak accompanies each session. The page states that progress is saved only in the browser and offers a table of ions. According to the listing, it also includes a projector mode designed for the interactive whiteboard, which cannot be seen in the screenshot.

For teachers, the resource can serve as independent practice for students or as a revision activity projected in class. It opens in the browser and shows no login or payment screen. Anyone teaching in Spanish should bear in mind that all the text, both the questions and the help, is in Catalan.

The work is shared under a CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 licence, which allows it to be adapted with attribution, for non-commercial use and with derivative works shared under the same licence.

New in the repository

FoQo generates organic formulation tests and gives the name of a drawn structure

FiQuiPedia’s app lets users practise organic chemistry nomenclature with configurable tests and a tool that goes from formula to name.

The resource

Name
FoQo - Formulación Química Orgánica
Authors
FiQuiPedia
Licence
AGPL v3 (code) · CC BY-SA 4.0 (content)

Open the resource · Its entry in the repository

FoQo, “Formulación Química Orgánica”, is a FiQuiPedia web app for practising the nomenclature of organic compounds. The repository listing places it in Secondary, Upper Secondary and University, within Science and Technology, and it is in Spanish. It is a page with no apparent registration: on opening, it shows three entry points directly, with no login form.

The start screen offers three buttons: “Tests”, “F → N” and “Panel”. According to the listing’s description, the tests ask users to name a compound from its formula or to write the formula for a given name, and the panel lets them configure the type of exercise: chains, double and triple bonds, radicals, branching, functional groups and rings. The “F → N” tool serves, again according to the listing, to obtain the name and properties of a formula or of a structure drawn in the editor. The page shows the palette of bonds and functional groups (-OH, -COOH, -NH₂, -NO₂ and others) used to build the molecules, and cites the JSME and 3Dmol.js libraries, which power the editor and the molecule viewer.

The listing says it supports several representations (condensed, skeletal, rectangular-scheme and 3D) and that users can consult both the 2013 IUPAC nomenclature, with the preferred name (PIN), and the 1993 one. For teachers, the listing notes that it can be used in class, for homework or as independent practice, with PDF export and as a SCORM package for Moodle. These features come from the listing’s description; the screenshot only shows the start screen.

The work carries a dual licence: AGPL v3 for the code and CC BY-SA 4.0 for the content. The page links to the code on GitLab and to the libraries it uses.

Community resource

A Geography teacher uses Gemini to build a counter of births and deaths around the world

“Pulso Demográfico Mundial en Tiempo Real”, by Ricardo Chao Prieto, simulates in real time the births and deaths in each country, based on the UN’s population projections for 2026.

Ricardo Chao Prieto, a teacher of Geography and History, has embedded the app in a post on Geografiando, the Year 9 Geography blog of the Agustinos Fathers’ school in León, and shared it in the group as made with Gemini. It starts from an estimated world population of about 8.326 billion people and keeps adding births, at an average of about 4.2 per second, and deaths, about 2 per second.

These are not real records but a statistical simulation using each country’s rates, as the app itself states. It shows the net growth accumulated during the session, lets users filter by continent, speed up by five or twenty times and pause, and includes a map and a table by country with its population, birth rate and death rate. The blog post accompanies it with the daily figures and a chart on the fall in the global fertility rate. For the classroom, it makes visible figures that are hard to picture when written down.
