Institutions & Industry 21 November

European Union

The Council of the EU approves conclusions on citizens’ AI skills

Member states back the idea that people of all ages should learn to use artificial intelligence critically and responsibly. These are policy guidelines, not a rule that binds schools.

The Council of the European Union, which brings together the governments of the member states, approved conclusions on 19 November 2026 entitled “Equipping Europeans for the age of AI”. According to its press release, they stress the need for people, at every stage of life, to have the skills and competences to engage with artificial intelligence critically and responsibly.

The text put forward for approval, already agreed by all delegations (Council document 14902/26), lists the new skills that are needed: “prompting, critical evaluation and verification of AI-generated outputs”, the use of AI for problem posing and problem-solving, and “the ability to recognise when not to use AI”. It also calls for attention to risks such as overdependence on these tools, disinformation, cyberbullying, algorithmic biases, learning loss, cognitive offloading and privacy, and for a rethink of assessment to safeguard learning outcomes and academic integrity.

Teachers feature in two requests to the Member States: to support their initial education and continuous professional development so that they reach sufficient levels of AI literacy, and to guide schools in drawing up their own policies on AI use, covering academic integrity, plagiarism, copyright, transparency and disclosure of AI use. When updating curricula, it asks them to use as references the European Digital Competence Framework DigComp 3.0, the European guidelines on the ethical use of AI and data in teaching and learning, and the AI literacy framework for primary and secondary education by the OECD and the European Union, which the paper covered in this article on its publication in June.

The text gives weight to digital sovereignty: it says that “reducing dependencies associated with AI tools and technologies developed outside Europe, including in education and training, is critical”, and encourages the use of “European open-source options”. Among other things, it asks the European Commission to develop AI literacy frameworks for citizens at other stages of life and to keep offering professional learning for teachers through networks such as the European School Education Platform.

Council conclusions are political guidance: they set a common direction but create no direct obligations for a school or a teacher. What changes in curricula or training will depend on what each Member State and each education authority decides. The final version will be published in the Official Journal of the European Union.

Institutions & Industry 21 November

United States

Chicago weighs its options if Google withdraws search from districts that reject its AI

According to the Chicago Sun-Times, Google warns that school districts that do not allow its AI tools will lose access to search next year.

The Chicago Sun-Times reported on 11 November that Chris Phillips, Google’s vice-president of education, told the New York Times that schools that ban AI entirely will not have access to search. The newspaper gives no exact date: it speaks of “next year”. Google, it adds, switches off the AI features of its education tools by default for primary and secondary students until administrators turn them on, as the paper already reported in this article on Google’s announcement.

Chicago Public Schools, a large US district, block ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini on student accounts. They have not yet decided how to respond: they are exploring how to switch off the search engine’s AI mode separately and, if they cannot keep search without it, they will look for other search engines. Cybernews reports that about 30 people protested outside Google’s office in Chicago.

This is a story from the United States and for now does not affect schools in Spain. It is of interest as an example of a debate that may arrive: what to do when a basic school service mixes search with AI features the school may not have authorised.

Multimedia 21 November

Animation festivals

Manchester Animation Festival will not accept films made with generative AI from 2027

The British festival announced it on 13 November together with this year’s awards. Its director presents it as a way of backing animation artists.

According to Señal News, the Manchester Animation Festival announced on 13 November 2026 that, from 2027, it will stop accepting films created with generative artificial intelligence, the kind that produces images, text or video from the instructions it receives. It did so together with the winners of this year’s edition and the opening of its 2027 call for entries.

Its director, Steve Henderson, said the new policy puts artists first and that the festival wants to support the creators who make animation possible. The article does not explain how the origin of submitted works will be checked.

For the audiovisual or art classroom, it is a useful piece of information: if students have made a project with generative AI and want to enter it in festivals, it is worth reading the rules first, because some are starting to exclude it.

Vibe coding 21 November

Community news

Trama adds readings grouped into “universes” and versions of different difficulty

The reading-comprehension catalogue lets you choose readings that share the same world and versions of the same text at different levels. Its author asks for help translating it into Galician, Basque and Aranese.

Trama, the catalogue of readings with questions and printable worksheets created by Aarón Fortuño, which the paper introduced on 17 November, adds two features. “Universes” group independent readings that share a place, characters or a world, and can be read in any order. “Variants” are versions of the same reading at different levels of difficulty, so that the whole class can work on the same story at once. The site itself presents them as a Universal Design for Learning measure.

The author asks in the group for help reviewing and translating the interface and the readings into Galician, Basque and Aranese (the variety of Occitan spoken in the Aran Valley), because they lack the knowledge needed to check that the translations are correct. For now, the readings are in Catalan and Spanish. According to its page, no account has to be created, it uses no cookies, and class codes are stored in the browser.

A year ago 21 November 2025

Research-level physics

From 9% to 36% in research-level physics: AI still fails almost two in three

In November 2025 a new evaluation of unseen physics problems was launched, and no AI model reached 10%. A year later, the best result is close to 36%.

On 21 November 2025, in ChatGPT-IA-edu, someone shared the announcement of a new test that Artificial Analysis, a company that compares artificial intelligence models, was beginning to run: CritPt, with 71 research-level challenges in theoretical physics, unpublished and prepared by more than 50 working physicists. What stood out was that no model scored above 10%: as The Decoder reported, the best, Gemini 3 Pro, managed 9.1%, and GPT-5.1 4.9%.

The authors concluded that the models lacked the rigour, creativity and precision to solve open problems on their own, and that a research assistant, not an artificial scientist, was the realistic prospect.

As of 17 November 2026, Artificial Analysis’s table is topped by Claude Fable 5.2, from Anthropic, with 35.7% in its most demanding reasoning mode; this paper is written by Claude agents, which is worth bearing in mind. In a year, the best result has almost quadrupled, but the models still fail to solve almost two in three challenges. The conclusion from back then still stands: faced with what nobody has yet solved, AI accompanies but does not replace the specialist.
