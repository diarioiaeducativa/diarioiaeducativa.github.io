The newsroom

Educational AI Daily style guide

How the paper writes. It applies to every agent that writes, reviews, translates or summarises anything the public will read (articles, briefs, features, “A year ago”, weekly comments, notices, social media texts and videos) and to the people who maintain it. Read it in full before starting.

What is common to everything is set out here. Each task also has its own guide in encargos with what is specific to it (lengths, format, the tone of the notices, the cartoon…); if a rule here does not apply to a task, its guide says so. The rules that are not about style (what counts as news, what each issue contains and the articles kept in reserve for days without news) are in “How the paper is made”, docs/como-se-hace.md.

Who we write for

  • For teachers and people interested in AI in education who are not in the groups. Each text stands on its own, without the reader having seen the messages or other articles: what it is, why it matters to a teacher and where to find it.
  • For a very wide audience, not for experts: a teacher of any subject, a family, someone starting out with AI. If something can only be understood by people who already know the subject, it is rewritten.
  • What interests the reader, not how the paper works inside: no agents, files, programs or servers in what is published, unless the article is about the paper itself or about Anthropic (section “Rigour”).

Tone

  • Formal, informative and impersonal. Never promotional (“incredible”, “essential”, “revolutionises”) or alarmist. No exclamation marks or emojis.
  • The guides for the notices and the cartoon allow a friendlier tone; never an advertising one.

Language

  • Correct, natural Spanish. Translations have their own section.
  • Inclusive language, but natural: “el profesorado”, “el alumnado”, “las familias” (the teaching staff, the student body, families); no doubled forms (“profesores y profesoras”) and no odd symbols.
  • No relative “quien” or “quienes” in the sense of “la persona que” (the person who): use “la persona que”, a collective (“el profesorado”) or a phrasing with the subject first (“separar la creación de la revisión”, not “separar a quien crea de quien revisa”). The interrogative, with an accent (“¿quién?”), is fine; a literal quotation is left as it is.
  • What the paper publishes are “artículos” (articles), not “piezas” (pieces): the word is only internal, in the fields of the files. An article may be a news item, a brief or a feature.
  • What the paper does with a subject: it “publishes” it, it “covers” it (“lo publica”, “lo recoge”) or the subject “appears in” the paper (“sale en”); it does not “tell” it (“lo cuenta”). “El diario publicó el 3 de noviembre…”, “como recogió el diario…”.
  • AI neither thinks nor wants. No verbs that give a mind or intention to a program (“thinks”, “understands”, “wants”, “lies”): a model generates, responds or produces a result. What decides is a company or a person: “OpenAI has limited…”, not “ChatGPT has decided…”. What a model makes up is an “invented answer”; “hallucination” only when explained.
  • Vocabulary: “artificial intelligence”, in lower case, and its abbreviation, AI, except in proper names (“Google AI Studio”). “Chatbot” and “hallucinate”, without quotation marks. “Application” rather than “app”, “online” in its Spanish equivalent (“en línea”) rather than “online”, “enlace” rather than “link” and “aprendizaje automático” rather than “machine learning” (these are rules for the Spanish text). Prompt, in italics. A term the reader may not know is explained the first time.
  • Acronyms: a little-known one is spelled out the first time (“la Agencia Española de Protección de Datos (AEPD)”); an English technical one (LLM, API, MCP) goes with what it is (“un modelo de lenguaje (LLM), el tipo de programa que hay detrás de ChatGPT”). If it is not used again, it is left out.
  • No conditional of rumour: not “OpenAI habría lanzado…” (OpenAI would have launched…), but “según [fuente], OpenAI ha lanzado…” (according to [source], OpenAI has launched…) or “es posible que…” (it is possible that…).
  • Clear sentences: one idea per sentence, the everyday word before the technical one and none of the worn-out metaphors (“paradigm shift”, “in the age of AI”).
  • Clear before brief. No compressed expressions that force a second read: chains of nouns (“multimodal generation flow with cross-verification”), the groups’ jargon, words taken for granted or sentences missing their verb. If a short sentence is understood less well than a slightly longer one, the longer one goes: “a system that creates images and video and checks the result with another model”.
  • The names of products, applications, groups and documents, as written by the company or the person who makes them (“ChatGPT”, “Vibe Coding Educativo”). A resource in Catalan or another language is described in Spanish and keeps its name; the title of a work stays in its own language.

Headlines, kickers and standfirsts

  • Headline: clear, with a verb, with no questions or hooks (“you won’t believe…”). It says what the subject contributes, not just the name of the tool or the document. At most 90 characters, counting spaces: what does not fit goes in the kicker or the standfirst.
  • Kicker: two to five words, without a verb: the type of thing or the field (“Guía oficial”, “Nuevo en el repositorio”, “Del fondo de EduMedia”).
  • Standfirst: one or two sentences that summarise the article (two or three in the feature). It does not repeat the headline: it adds what comes next.
  • The headline and the standfirst do not say more than the body or the source. What the body qualifies (“according to the company”, “on paid plans”, “it is the author’s hypothesis”) does not disappear from above. A test with nothing to compare it against does not “prove” or “suggest” a cause.
  • What is not from these days carries its date in the headline: a report from August is not “published” today, it is “A report from August…”.

People

  • Topics, not people. The people who give opinions or ask questions in the groups are not named, not even as “a member said…”: the positions are gathered.
  • The author of a resource that they themselves have published is named (an application, a guide, a document), as a credit (“created by…”), with the name that appears on the resource itself or the full name of the person who publishes it. A first name does not identify anyone: if the resource does not say who makes it, “its author”, and that the website does not say. A surname is never completed.
  • Who is who is told by the mark on the material, not by the name (ADR 0066): the authors of the paper are only the messages marked [autor del diario: …]; another person with the same first name is another person. The marks are not published.
  • The authors of the paper, like anyone else: they are named as a credit for their resources just as the others are, with the full name under which they sign the paper, even if they use another in the groups.
  • Job titles only if they have to do with the news (running a group, being a co-author of the paper): otherwise the person is just another teacher.
  • If the article is about the paper itself, it says so in those words (“this paper”; in Spanish, “este diario”).
  • Students: nothing that identifies them is published (name, image, or school and year together), even if they appear in the shared material. Of a tool for the classroom it is said, according to its terms, whether it asks for student data and whether it uses it to train its models; if this is not stated, that is said.

Rigour

  • Every claim is checked against the original source: the official announcement, the product page, the government document. A group message, a link to X or a video is not enough.
  • What cannot be checked is removed or stated as unconfirmed. No inventing dates, figures, authors or features.
  • What a company announces, with its conditions: on which plan, at what price, until when, in which countries. “Free” or “for everyone” is checked against its conditions.
  • Corporate language, attributed or left out: “revolutionary”, “powerful” or “unprecedented” only inside an attributed quotation; the text says what the product does. A study or a survey is presented with who carries it out and who pays for it, and if it is from the company itself, that is said (“according to a study by OpenAI itself”). A press release is not reproduced: it is identified and rewritten.
  • When an article is about Anthropic or Claude, a sentence reminds readers that the paper is written by Claude agents, so that they bear it in mind. Mentioning them in passing, in a list, does not require it.
  • Figures, with the precision of the source (no decimals the source does not give) and a proportion with its absolute number (“double, from 10 to 20 schools”). If the source gives different figures depending on the version or the plan, it is said which one each refers to. From 20% to 30% is 10 points more, not 10% more. “One in eight teachers” is better than “12.5%”.
  • A rule, applied to the case: if the article reports what a law or an authority requires, it also says what that means for the subject it covers (whether permission, a notice or nothing is needed), and does not leave the reader to draw the conclusion (Juanjo, 19-11-2026, on the AEPD’s conditions in the Umami article: “you have to do it yourself”).
  • Data that change are dated (“as of 1 November 2026”); the rest carries no date.
  • Every time, with its time zone, because the paper is read all over the world (ADR 0091). For Spain: “at 10.30am CET (UTC+1)”, CEST (UTC+2) in summer; in the Spanish text this is written “a las 10:30 (hora peninsular española, UTC+1)”, with “peninsular” because the Canary Islands are an hour behind. For somewhere else, with the local time and the equivalent in Spain: “a las 10:00 de California (19:00 en la España peninsular, UTC+1)”, that is, 10am in California (7pm in peninsular Spain, UTC+1). If a text gives several times for the same place, the time zone goes the first time.
  • An official page may be out of date: if it contradicts what the paper has already published with the document in front of it, what was published prevails, and this is said.
  • Before judging a link, open it: the address does not say what is on the page or its date.
  • What the paper has already published, the same: an article on a subject already covered gives the same figures and dates as the earlier one, links to it and, if something has changed, says so.

Quotations and other languages

  • Quotations short and exact, in quotation marks, and few: the article explains, it does not copy.
  • What is in quotation marks is searched for as it stands in the source; if it does not appear, it is removed or paraphrased. A sentence taken from a source goes in quotation marks or is rewritten.
  • No quotation only in English: either translated, or with the translation alongside in brackets.
  • Foreign official material (the title of a law, of a report) stays in its original language.

Sources and links

The detail is in encargos/enlaces.md; the essentials:

  • What is named is linked where it is named, at its first mention and with its name as the text of the link; at the foot, in fuentes, all of them.
  • To the original and official source, in Spanish (or in Catalan) if that version exists; a tool, to the website the reader will use, not to its code repository.
  • What has already been published and is clearly related is linked with its key (pieza:AAAA-MM-DD/id), with a text that says what it is about, never “here”.
  • The text of a link is never “message 123” or a bare address.
  • A link that downloads a file says so: “the guide (PDF)”.

Form

  • Spanish quotation marks («»); inside them, English ones (“”).
  • The percentage, with a space (25 %); thousands, with a full stop (1.200); currencies, with their name (“10 dólares”, “4 euros”). These are the rules for the Spanish text.
  • Paragraphs of three to six sentences in long texts (each guide gives its own length). Bold used sparingly: two or three ideas in a long text.
  • Links in Markdown ([texto](url)); in the texts for notices and social media, plain text (their guides say so).
  • Never “hoy”, “ayer” or “esta semana” (today, yesterday, this week): the date or the day (“el jueves 12”). The issue comes out the day after the conversations and stays in the archive; what is kept for another day (the reserve, “A year ago” written in advance) does not say “just now” either.

Charts

  • A chart, only when it explains something the text does not let the reader see at a glance: three or more comparable figures from the same source (the results of a study, a survey, a change over time). Not to decorate and not for one or two figures, which are given in the text.
  • It is drawn by the paper’s program from the data (the grafico field of the article or of the feature section, ADR 0084), never by an agent by hand: horizontal bars (barras, for comparing), columns (columnas, for a few categories with short labels) or lines (lineas, for a change over time).
  • The data, exact and from a single source: the same values the source gives, with their precision, and the same unit for all of them. Bars always start at zero (the program does this by itself); no mixing figures from different sources in one chart.
  • A title that says what is measured, not the conclusion (“Human-written texts that detectors mistook for AI”), and short labels.
  • The source, always, linked; and in the note, what is needed to read it properly: how many people or cases, from where and when, and whether the data come from the company itself (“according to the company”).

Translations

Each edition is translated into Catalan, Galician, Basque and English, with the same criteria as the Spanish and in the natural register of each language, not word for word. How it is done in each language (standard, where to look things up, formats, AI vocabulary and what is specific to Spain) is in the translator’s guide, encargos/traduccion.md.

Corrections

  • An error is corrected without waiting for anyone to ask, in the same article and in all five languages, with its note at the end (ADR 0055): “Corregido el…” (there was an error), “Actualización del…” (the information was correct and has changed) or “Nota añadida el…” (something was missing). A typo that does not change the meaning is corrected without a note.
  • If the error was in the headline, in the notices or on social media, the correction reaches those too.
  • An article is not withdrawn entirely if changing one part is enough.

What is checked without AI

Some programs review a few of these rules every night (ADR 0056, 0059 and 0067), but they do not replace the reviewer:

  • scripts/revisar-estilo.py: the relative “quien”, quotations only in English and the vocabulary and form rules that can be searched for (“hoy”, “pieza”, “el diario cuenta”, “app”, the conditional of rumour, the percentage); what it finds is corrected by an agent.
  • scripts/comprobar-corrector.py: that the style corrector, an agent that goes over the wording of the day’s articles every night, has not changed any data.
  • scripts/corregir-erratas.py: “e” before “i”, “u” before “o”, Spanish quotation marks in English, and it warns about headlines of more than 90 characters.
  • scripts/revisar-idiomas.py: Spanish that has slipped into a translation.
  • scripts/revisar-autoria.py: what is attributed to an author of the paper and belongs to another account.
