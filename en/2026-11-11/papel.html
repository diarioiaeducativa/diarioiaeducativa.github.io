Multimedia 12 November

Scientific drawing

Three models draw an animal cell diagram in SVG with no content errors

A teacher asked three advanced models for a diagram of an animal cell. None made content errors, and they put the reliability down to the vector format.

In the group, members compared the results of asking Claude Fable 5.2, an OpenAI model and a Google model for a teaching diagram of an animal cell in SVG format, the vector drawing format. According to the person who ran the test, all three delivered a diagram with the correct labels; Claude's was done in a few minutes, ChatGPT's shortly afterwards, and Gemini took close to half an hour.

The explanation offered is that, in a vector drawing, the language model itself writes the drawing and draws on everything it knows about the subject. Bitmap image generators, by contrast, receive a description and pass it to another model with less knowledge, and that is where the mistakes appear. This is the author's hypothesis; they say they are convinced of it but have yet to test it with other models, so it is not an established result.

They preferred Claude's sober, clear style, while the other two seemed worse as illustrations: in one, elements overlapped and it was impossible to tell where the arrows went. In the discussion that followed, someone pointed out that the hard part today is knowing which tool to choose for each task, and another member said they already ask AI to make the images in their presentations SVG. It is the line the paper already reported on creating infographics in HTML and SVG.

For the classroom, the practical rule that emerges is to ask for SVG where accuracy matters, and to check the diagrams anyway before using them.

Vibe coding 12 November

Coding with Fable

Anthropic recommends telling Fable 5.2 the code is its own if you want it to reorganise it

Anthropic's guide to Fable 5.2 explains that the model behaves differently depending on who is named as the owner of the code, at a cost in tokens.

A thread in the group shared an article from Xataka about a guideline in Anthropic's guide to prompting Claude Fable 5.2: the model pays close attention to who owns the code it is working on.

According to the guide, in the company's tests on small programming tasks, if the prompt says nothing about the owner, or presents the code as someone else's, the model tends to add to what exists even if it duplicates code. If it is told it is the long-term owner and maintainer, it merges the repeated code into a shared function in around 75% of runs, compared with under 10% without that sentence. The figures come from the company itself.

The guide also warns of the price: output tokens rose by between 70% and 80%, the model made changes nobody had asked for (mostly tests and documentation) and occasionally altered the behaviour of a function. For that reason it recommends not using the sentence when the code belongs to the user and the aim is to touch as little as possible, and checking the summary of changes, which may leave out small modifications.

For anyone building educational apps with agents, the use is to decide, case by case, whether they want an assistant that respects the existing structure or one that improves it.

Multimedia 12 November

Educational video

A second video with Fable 5.2 adds an agent that checks the scientific content before generating it

After the photosynthesis animation, the same teacher has made one on cellular respiration with a reviewing agent, and has translated the first into Catalan.

After the photosynthesis animation that Fable 5.2 prepared from a single prompt, its author has published another on cellular respiration. This time they asked a scientific reviewing agent to check the text before it was turned into audio and the images before the video was rendered, to avoid errors. The cellular respiration videos were made with the console version of Claude Code.

They also said they made another video on the same topic with Opus 5.5, without a reviewer, whose quality seemed much poorer; they did not even watch all of it, so they do not know whether it contains errors. The comparison is not controlled, because the model and the reviewer change at the same time.

Separately, they translated the photosynthesis video into Catalan. The voice, from Gemini, seems to them not as good as the Spanish one and has a few small flaws, although the overall result is very good.

Anyone wanting to do the same has two practical ideas here: include a content-checking step before producing, and try translation as a way of reusing the material in another language.

Multimedia 12 November

Cinema with AI

“Polvo eres”, a stop-motion animated short made with generative AI

A member of the group has shared their first short film, a spoof of cute horror made with generative AI.

“Polvo eres”, by Orestes G. Villanueva, is a short film of about two and a half minutes that parodies horror cinema with a colony of little dust monsters living under a sofa and a vacuum cleaner patrolling at night.

According to the video's description, it was made with Google Flow (image and video with Veo 3.1 and Nano Banana), with music from Flow Music, AI-generated voices and effects, and script and editing assisted by Claude. It includes a notice that all the content is generated and carries SynthID watermarks. It has subtitles in Spanish and English.

It could serve as an example of a student project or of animated film-making in the classroom, with the transparency about the use of AI that the video itself shows.

Vibe coding 12 November

Community resource

Eduvers brings together interactive Physics and Chemistry simulations and is preparing 3D virtual labs

Its author is adding simulations to the website and announces that he will link 3D labs in the coming weeks.

The Physics and Chemistry simulations section of Eduvers offers 19 simulations, including free fall, inclined plane, projectile motion, Hooke's law, waves, geometric optics, the 3D heating curve and the seasons in Physics, and isotopes, the ideal gas law and volumetric analysis in Chemistry. They can be filtered by area and topic.

Its author explains in the Vibe Coding Educativo group that he makes them with the help of AI models and that he will publish more in the coming weeks, with 3D virtual labs. He warns that there are currently errors that he will correct and complete.

Another member of the group replied with their own collection of resources for sixth-form students, and the author added that you can ask AI for an interactive 3D practical of what is done in the laboratory.

A year ago 12 November 2025

Language models

GPT-5.1 arrived in ChatGPT to amazement at its speed and did not last four months there

In November 2025, people were struck by how quickly GPT-5.1, OpenAI’s “more conversational” model, arrived. By 11 March 2026 it had already disappeared from ChatGPT.

On 12 November 2025, in ChatGPT-IA-edu, OpenAI’s announcement was shared: GPT-5.1 was reaching all ChatGPT users that week, smarter, more reliable and more conversational. The reaction was amazement at the speed of the rollout.

A month later, on 11 December, GPT-5.2 came out, and on 5 March 2026, GPT-5.4. Six days after that, according to OpenAI’s page on retired models, GPT-5.1 Instant, Thinking and Pro left ChatGPT, and their conversations and custom GPTs moved to GPT-5.3 Instant and GPT-5.4 Thinking or Pro. They remain only in the API.

For the classroom, the lesson goes beyond a name: a custom GPT or a set of instructions fine-tuned for a certain tone can end up tied to a model that, a few months later, is no longer there. What a year ago was the news of the week is today a line in a list of retirements.
