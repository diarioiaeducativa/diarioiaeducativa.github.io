Debates Monday, 23 November

Authorship and assessment

An imagined case asks who signs a prize-winning story illustrated with AI

An instalment of the series “Familias con inteligencia” imagines a case: a student declares that they used AI, wins a competition and a classmate challenges it. Chema Falcó asks schools for clear criteria.

A new instalment of the series “Familias con inteligencia”, titled “¿Quién firma?” (“Who signs?”) and written by Chema Falcó, was shared in ChatGPT-IA-edu. It sets out an imagined case, which its author presents as generic: a student in the third year of ESO (compulsory secondary education, ages 14 to 15) writes a story, generates the illustrations with an AI tool and declares that they used it. The rules of the school’s literary competition ask for “original works” and do not exclude AI, so the student wins first prize. A classmate who has illustrated her entry by hand questions whether that counts as drawing.

The text sets out two views of the case: the student’s, who argues that the story and every decision about the images are theirs, and the classmate’s, who maintains that generating images is not drawing. Falcó concludes that the authorship of the story belongs to the student and that directing (choosing, discarding, organising and retouching) is a creative act, but a different one from executing. It is therefore worth recognising both contributions without confusing them.

As criteria, the instalment proposes drafting rules clear enough to leave no ambiguity about the use of AI, and asking students to declare that use and be able to show the process. For families, it suggests working out together what the child invented, what they decided and what they delegated to the tool. In the group, Falcó raised the underlying question: whether assessment systems distinguish between execution, creative direction and authorship, or only value the final result.

The paper has already covered other instalments of the series: on declaring the use of AI and on the percentage from a detector that proves nothing. The case in this instalment is a scenario set out by its author, not a verified fact.

Vibe coding Monday, 23 November

Community app

An app turns spoken answers into assessment reports for families

“Informes a viva voz”, by Ernesto Boixader, asks teachers to answer three or seven questions about each student, and AI writes the report from those answers.

Ernesto Boixader presented “Informes a viva voz” in Vibe Coding Educativo, an app he created for assessment reports. According to his description, teachers answer questions about each student, by voice or in writing, with one version of three questions and another of seven. From those answers AI writes a report adapted to the subject, the year group and the intended reader, and the teacher reviews it and decides the final version.

The app’s page, under the name “A viva voz”, offers three links: one for ChatGPT, one for Claude and one for Gemini. In other words, the report is generated by the AI of whichever service each teacher chooses.

Before using it with real data, it is worth bearing in mind that the page does not say what student data is requested and says nothing about privacy. Whatever is written about a student will be processed by the chosen service, and each school should check its terms, especially if the text includes names or data on health or personal circumstances.

Multimedia Monday, 23 November

Cinema with AI

Raül Torres releases his short film “Buzz” and explains step by step how he made it

The director explains on a web page and in a video how he shot it by hand in virtual reality and left only the developing of the images to AI.

Raül Torres shared in EduMedia-IAG “Buzz”, the short film that the paper had already covered in a preview and in its workflow. According to the video’s description, it is a war and horror short shot with a Quest 3 headset on a 3D set.

The making-of, on a web page and in a video, explains six steps: a script designed for continuity, the basic set in Blender, shooting with the headset, turning those takes into shots with Seedance 2.5, sound with ElevenLabs, and editing and colour in DaVinci Resolve. The idea he defends is that AI does not direct the film: where each character is, how long a shot lasts and how the camera moves are decided by hand, and AI only develops that image, as a laboratory develops a negative.

The video ends with four tips from the author: whatever is not on the set, AI will invent; it is best to shoot by hand to give the camera an intention; frames from the edit itself should be used as a reference; and each flaw should be fixed where it costs least, rhythm in the edit and light in the colour grade. For the classroom, it works as an example of a production workflow in which students decide the shot before generating it.

An editor that turns ABC notation into a score and plays it in the browser

María Teresa González created a simple music editor: you type the melody as text, the score appears and you can listen to it, with nothing to install.

The resource

Name
Editor Musical Interactivo con Audio
Authors
María Teresa González
Licence
Creative Commons BY-SA (Attribution-ShareAlike)

Open the resource · Its entry in the repository

“Editor Musical Interactivo con Audio” is a web page by María Teresa González, shared under a Creative Commons BY-SA (Attribution-ShareAlike) licence, according to the repository entry. Its author presented it in the Vibe Coding Educativo group in June 2025, published on GitHub and Netlify. According to the entry, it is intended for primary and secondary education, in the area of Arts and Humanities, and it is in Spanish.

When you open it, there is a text box on the left where the music is written in ABC notation, a text format that describes a melody with letters and bar lines. The page comes with an example already written, a C major scale with its title, time signature and key. On the right, under “Partitura generada” (generated score), the stave is drawn with the treble clef, the 4/4 time signature and the notes.

Above the score there are two audio controls, “Reproducir” (play) and “Parar” (stop), with a tempo slider (120 beats per minute when it opens) and a volume slider (75%). Below the text box there are buttons to generate the score and, in addition, others labelled “1 Línea”, “2 Líneas”, “3 Líneas”, “4 Líneas” and “Ritmo”. The screenshot does not show exactly what each of these last ones does, so it is worth trying them out before planning a lesson.

For a music teacher, the most valuable thing is that students can write, listen to, correct and listen again to their own melody without needing instruments or a complex editing program. According to the entry, which reports what its author says, it can be used with the interactive whiteboard for melodic dictation, collective composition, correcting scores that contain errors or transcribing rhythmic fragments.

The drawback is that it requires learning ABC notation, which in secondary education can be one more item of classroom content, but in primary education will need teachers to give guidance or prepare starting melodies. It is used directly in the browser, with no visible account on the page. A modest resource, but a useful one for bringing the stave and sound to any classroom with a computer.

A year ago Sunday, 23 November 2025

Apps without coding

Google offered Opal for building apps without coding; a year later it has shut it down

In November 2025, Google offered Opal for free, a tool for building apps with AI without knowing how to code. It shut it down on 17 November 2026, and what people made with it does not move to Gemini on its own.

On 23 November 2025, in Vibe Coding Educativo, a Google post in Spanish was shared that presented Opal as a way to “create your own apps with AI, without knowing how to code”. That same day, someone was still asking how to view the code of an HTML file. Opal promised to skip that step: all you had to do was describe the app.

In December, Google brought it to the Gemini app as an experimental Gem, a customised Gemini assistant, according to its blog. A year later it no longer exists: according to the Opal FAQ, Google shut it down on 17 November 2026 and says its function has moved to Gemini Skills, saved instructions that you invoke by typing “/” and their name.

The move is not automatic. Gems become Skills on their own, as the paper reported with the renaming of EduGems to EduPrompts, but Opal apps no longer work and shared ones will not open. Their files remain in Google Drive with the original prompts, which can be copied into a new Skill; Opal could chain instructions, web searches and images or video in a single app. Teachers who used it in class will have to recreate them as Skills.
